# What Collides in the Dark

Two galaxies are approaching each other. Between them they carry hundreds of billions of stars. Almost every star will miss.

Yet neither galaxy will emerge as it was.

For now, each has a shape that can be recognised from far away: a bright centre, winding arms, dark lanes threaded through the light. Each has taken billions of years to acquire that shape. Stars have formed, aged and scattered their material. New stars have grown among the old. Vast clouds drift between them, containing the ingredients for lights that do not yet exist.

The two galaxies are bound to one another. This encounter will not be a single passing visit. Their gravity has already begun changing the paths of stars, long before their bright outlines meet.

On the near side of one galaxy, an arm lengthens. Stars that once travelled around its centre are drawn into a widening curve. Farther out, the curve becomes a stream, reaching into the darkness between the galaxies. The other galaxy stretches too. Each pulls unevenly on the other, distorting something that seemed almost permanent.

No stars need to touch for a spiral to come apart.

The centres draw closer. From a distance, the approaching lights seem packed so tightly that there can be no way through. But a galaxy's apparent solidity is an effect of looking across an immense depth. Between one star and the next there is room for whole planetary systems, and then far more room beyond them.

The stars pass into one another's territory.

They do not strike a wall of suns. Most continue shining, following paths bent by the changing pull around them. Some will be flung far from the place where they were born. Others will remain near the centre of the growing disturbance. Their destinations change while their fires continue.

The collision is happening elsewhere.

Through the spaces between those stars extend clouds of gas. They are faint, cold in places, and spread across distances that dwarf the stars themselves. Two streams of this material converge. They cannot pass through each other as the widely separated stars can. Gas meets gas. The encounter drives a shock through it, compressing and heating it.

A region that scarcely showed in the galaxy's light has become the place where its future is being decided.

Follow a part of that gas. Before the encounter, it was spread too thinly to gather into stars. Now it is crowded against more of itself. Heat resists the gathering: hot gas pushes outward. But energy escapes as radiation, and in some dense regions the gas cools enough for gravity to draw it farther inward.

One gathering divides into many.

Within the cloud, separate knots grow denser. Each draws in nearby material. Falling gas becomes hot again, but now it is accumulating beneath the weight of a growing body. At the centres of these bodies, the pressure and temperature become great enough for the centres of hydrogen atoms to join. The new reactions release energy. The knots begin to shine from within.

The galaxy's old stars have mostly missed one another. In the material between them, new stars are being made.

At first, much of this light is hidden. Dust absorbs it and warms, giving out a different light, beyond the colours visible to an eye. The apparent dark lanes contain a brilliance that an ordinary view would conceal. The most dramatic part of the collision is happening behind its own curtain.

Then the young stars begin changing that curtain.

Their radiation heats surrounding gas. Fierce flows of material push outward. Cavities open in the cloud; dense ridges remain between them. Here the gas resists. There it is driven away. The stars are dispersing the material that made them, and with it some of the supply from which more stars could have formed.

The new brightness carries its own limit.

Meanwhile the galaxies move apart again. Their centres separate, trailing long streams behind them. For a while, there seem to be two galaxies once more, badly torn but escaping.

Their encounter has changed more than their outlines. Energy from their motion around each other has been passed into the movements of stars and the vast surrounding matter, much of it invisible. The two centres cannot regain their former separation. Gravity draws them back.

They meet again.

More gas is crowded inward. More stars form. The old stellar paths are rearranged once more. Through successive passages, the distinction between the two systems becomes harder to sustain. A star that began in one now crosses among stars from the other. There is no border left to cross.

In this encounter, the two bright centres finally settle into one. Around them spreads a broad, rounded galaxy. It contains the stars of both former spirals, moving in a multitude of directions, and a younger population born during their collision. Faint streams still extend beyond its brighter body. They are the unfinished edges of the event.

The great rush of star formation subsides. Much of the readily available cold gas has gone into stars or been heated and driven away. The brightest newborn stars spend their fuel quickly. Others will shine for far longer. The new galaxy gradually loses the fierce appearance of its making.

From a distance, it can look tranquil.

Among its stars is one that formed in the cloud we followed through the encounter. The original cloud has dispersed, but this star is only at the beginning of its long life. Around it move older stars that once belonged to separate spirals. Their paths now carry them through the same immense system.

The two great spirals are gone. Most of their stars remain. Between the old lights shine others that belonged to neither galaxy, made from the material caught between them.
