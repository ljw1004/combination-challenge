# What the Darkness Kept

The universe is going dark with fuel still inside it.

The clouds that once gave birth to stars have dwindled. Much of their matter is locked in bodies that will never return it; much of the rest is too thinly spread to gather. The great blue stars are gone. So are generations of quieter ones. Yet hydrogen remains, carrying the same possibility that lit the first stars. Darkness has arrived without using it all.

In one possible late chapter of this universe, two small bodies circle each other. Between them is enough hydrogen to sustain a star for trillions of years.

Neither is a star.

They formed while the galaxies were still bright, in a cloud that broke into separate concentrations under its own gravity. Gas fell toward both growing centres. The falling matter grew hot; the objects shone. Around them, heavier newborn stars gathered still more gas and compressed their interiors until hydrogen nuclei began joining in the reactions that make helium. Those stars could replace the energy escaping from their surfaces. Their light had a continuing source.

The two smaller bodies stopped growing too soon.

Each held about a twentieth of the Sun's mass. Gathered into one sufficiently hot body, most of their combined mass could have sustained a small star. Divided between two centres, it was insufficient. Gravity squeezed them, but another kind of pressure became important: the resistance of densely crowded electrons, governed by quantum rules. This pressure could support them even as they cooled. Their centres never became hot enough to sustain the ordinary hydrogen fusion of stars.

They were brown dwarfs. In their youth they could fuse a little heavy hydrogen, a rare variety with an extra neutron. That brief supply ran out. Most of their hydrogen remained untouched. Their glow diminished as the heat of their formation escaped.

Nearby, the massive stars spent their fuel furiously. Some tore themselves apart, throwing newly made elements back into space. Other clouds gathered those elements into new generations. Stars were born, burned and died while the brown dwarfs continued circling, cooling, losing the little light they had.

The smallest true stars lasted far longer. Their interiors circulated, bringing fresh hydrogen into the hot central regions. They shone through ages in which galaxies changed beyond recognition. Eventually, even these economical stars approached the ends of their fuel supplies. New births became rare. The distinction between what had burned out and what had never properly begun grew difficult to see.

The pair belonged to the second kind.

Their hydrogen had survived the age of stars because they had been unable to use it. But survival alone would accomplish nothing. Left separately, each could continue cooling with enough nuclear energy inside it to sustain an immense history of light, and no way to begin that history.

They were still orbiting.

Every revolution sent faint gravitational waves outward: ripples in spacetime carrying energy away from the pair. The loss was almost imperceptible. Over the long stellar ages, it accumulated. The orbit contracted. The bodies moved closer and faster, releasing more energy as their mutual gravity bound them more tightly.

Their motion, unlike their old warmth, had not simply faded. The very process that drained energy from the orbit was bringing the two reserves of hydrogen together.

At first, the shortening of a revolution required an expanse of time in which whole populations of stars could change. Later, the change accelerated. Each body raised tides in the other. Their shapes stretched along the line between their centres. The division into two self-contained worlds became harder to maintain.

Gas began to cross it.

These two were nearly equal in mass. Their exchange did not settle into a lasting flow from one body to the other. It ran away. Matter poured across, struck, heated; the separate structures unravelled. Gas streamed outward, taking some of the pair's mass and spin with it. The rest joined in a swollen, violently disturbed object.

Light returned at once.

That first brightness came largely from motion turned into heat. It could have ended there. An impact can make matter shine without making a star. Enough gas thrown away, too little heat reaching the interior, a different encounter: the result could still have been another body destined to cool.

Here, enough remained.

The merged object radiated the violence of its birth into space. It settled and contracted. Now the weight pressing inward was nearly the weight of both former bodies together. Its hot interior was compressed under a burden neither had possessed alone.

Hydrogen nuclei, stripped of their electrons, encountered one another in increasing numbers. Most encounters accomplished nothing. The nuclei repelled one another electrically. But at the temperatures and densities deep inside this object, a small fraction could cross that barrier through quantum tunnelling. A slow chain of reactions joined hydrogen into helium, releasing energy.

The reactions became sufficient to replace what the surface lost.

A star had begun.

Its continuing light was far fainter than the outburst of the merger. It would never rival the great stars that had shone when the pair formed. Its small mass made a quieter future possible: slow fusion, a modest loss of energy, hydrogen circulated through its interior. The time ahead of it could be measured in trillions of years.

Around it, old remnants kept cooling. The expanding universe kept stretching the light already travelling through it. This new star did not restore the vanished clouds or summon back the vanished generations. Its own hydrogen, too, would eventually be spent.

Yet an immense interval now lay between ignition and exhaustion. The same matter that had remained dim through the brilliance of galaxies would shine through a stretch of their darkness.

For all those earlier ages, the two bodies had held both the fuel and the gravity. The fuel was divided. Gravity bound them together, while their orbital motion kept them apart. Only when that arrangement ended could another begin.

Their first formation had left two cold futures.

Their joining made a long one full of light.
