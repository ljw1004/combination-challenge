# Before the Cloud Grew Thin

Deep inside the star, the outward shock had stopped.

Above it, a ragged band of gas was falling inward. Oxygen and silicon travelled together in that band, crowded among the other products of the star's burning. Below lay the collapsed core. Between them, the shock trembled under the weight of descending matter.

The star had spent its life making larger atomic nuclei from smaller ones. The released energy had helped support its immense body. But the centre had reached a composition from which further joining could yield no sustaining heat. It had collapsed, and the sudden halt of its innermost part had launched this shock into the falling star.

Now the shock was losing. It spent energy breaking nuclei apart while more material pressed down from above. The oxygen-rich band descended towards a region where even the elements the star had made could be dismantled.

Energy poured from the crushed core as neutrinos: particles able to cross enormous depths of matter. Most passed through. A small fraction gave up energy behind the shock, heating gas that rose and pressed outward. The falling layers were uneven, disturbed by currents that had run through them before the collapse. Their uneven arrival stirred the heated region further.

The shock bulged.

Hot gas rose into the bulge. The outward movement spread. Before the whole ragged band could fall through, the shock drove into it, crushing and heating it, changing some of its nuclei again. Then the gas began to move the other way.

Its distance from the core increased.

The shock crossed the layers above. At the distant surface, the star broke into sudden brilliance. Behind that advancing front, the band stretched and tore into streams and knots. The enclosing weight diminished as everything around it expanded. The crushed core remained behind, a neutron star; this portion of the former interior was moving fast enough to escape.

It was still too hot to become anything solid.

Throughout its confinement in the star, its oxygen and silicon had existed among violently moving particles. Even where electrons could surround nuclei to make atoms, the heat prevented lasting mineral structures. The ingredients of rock had been present without a single grain of rock among them.

Now the streams lengthened. The knots swelled. Gas that had occupied a narrow region inside the star spread through a volume larger than the star itself. Expansion cooled it; radiation carried more energy away. The movement that had removed the gas from the core was also bringing it towards temperatures at which atoms could remain joined.

But that same movement was separating them.

In the thinner reaches of the broken band, an atom travelled farther between encounters. More space opened around every possible partner. Cooling made a lasting bond more likely when suitable atoms met; expansion made the meeting less likely to happen.

The gas could become cold enough for a solid and too sparse to assemble one.

Within a denser knot, encounters remained frequent. Silicon met oxygen. Some encounters left bonded pairs, molecules that moved through the gas together. Heat and energetic particles broke many apart. Others lasted, and further reactions built small clusters. An assembly could grow through several encounters, lose part of itself, and begin growing again.

The knot kept swelling throughout. There was no pause in its outward journey while these structures formed.

Where a cluster held together, the next collision could add to something already present. Clusters met other clusters. Some stuck. Chemical bonds connected more atoms into an irregular solid, with an interior and a surface exposed to the surrounding gas.

The star had made the elements by altering their nuclei. Here, the nuclei retained their identities while the atoms joined through their electrons. Oxygen was still oxygen. Silicon was still silicon. Their new arrangement could persist in a place where the heat of the star could no longer undo it.

A mineral grain moved inside the knot.

Around it, the expansion continued to draw unbound material apart. The grain's atoms remained close. Each was connected to neighbours within the solid; the spreading of the surrounding gas no longer determined the distances between them.

Other grains formed nearby. They did not appear everywhere together. One part of the knot was still too warm; another contained molecules that would not build the same solids. Farther out, the sparse streams carried unused ingredients through steadily increasing intervals of space. Within the crowded regions, dust accumulated through encounters that were becoming harder to repeat.

The former band was now a broken cloud of gas and grains. Its densest portions had assembled solids while there were still enough collisions to do so. Much of the material remained gaseous. As the cloud thinned, further growth slowed.

Yet the new grains were already changing something much larger than themselves.

Light from the glowing gas passed into the dusty knots. Some emerged; some was absorbed. A grain warmed by that energy released it again at longer wavelengths, beyond visible light. Across the remnant, brightness began to depend on what lay between one region and another.

A luminous patch on the far side of the cloud grew dim behind a concentration of dust. The gas there was still shining. Its light was being intercepted by solids made from material that had almost fallen into the collapsed core.

The dust spread across the glow in uneven streaks. Through gaps, the bright interior remained visible. Behind thicker patches, it disappeared. At infrared wavelengths the absorbing grains shone in their turn, carrying away energy received from the ruined star.

The ragged band had lost its old boundaries. Its pieces were moving apart through a widening remnant. But within those pieces, atoms had acquired boundaries of their own, small enough to persist amid the dispersal.

And where enough of them lay together, a dark seam crossed the light.
