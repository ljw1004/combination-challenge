# What the Darkness Kept

Almost every light in this galaxy is old. One is new.

It is a small red star, faint beside the brilliant stars that once filled the galaxy. Those stars have long since vanished. Their remnants remain, cooling or hidden in darkness. The great clouds from which new generations were born have dwindled. Through most of this galaxy, the making of stars has ceased.

Yet here, hydrogen is beginning to become helium. Energy travels outward from the centre of a newly kindled star. Its light could continue for trillions of years.

This is one possible scene in the far future. The star is not drawing on a new source of fuel. Its hydrogen was present throughout the galaxy's bright history. It survived all those generations without being used.

Where was it kept?

Long before the new star appeared, two smaller bodies occupied this part of the galaxy. They circled each other, each holding a great store of hydrogen. They had enough material between them to make a star. But that material was divided, and the division mattered.

A star's gravity presses its interior into conditions where nuclear reactions can sustain its light. The two separate bodies could not reach those conditions. Their inward squeeze was resisted by densely crowded electrons, even as their interiors cooled. They could remain enormous reservoirs of hydrogen without ever establishing a lasting hydrogen fire.

They were brown dwarfs.

To find their beginning, we must return to a galaxy still full of young stars. Clouds were gathering under their own gravity, breaking into denser regions. Gas fell toward these growing centres. The falling material grew hot and shone. Some of the new bodies accumulated enough matter for sustained fusion to take over from the heat of gathering. Others stopped growing too soon.

Two of those smaller bodies became our pair. Each had only about half the mass their eventual star would retain. They were almost twins, formed from the same cloud, already bound together. Gas around them dispersed. There was no longer enough nearby material falling inward to complete either one as a star.

Their first light faded.

Elsewhere, the brightest stars changed quickly. They had much more fuel, but used it at a prodigious rate. Some ended in explosions, returning altered material to surrounding space. Clouds inherited that material. More stars formed. The galaxy continued its long exchange between gathering and dispersal.

The brown dwarfs took little part in that exchange. They cooled, with most of their ordinary hydrogen still present. A brief youthful use of a rarer form of hydrogen had scarcely touched the much larger reserve.

Around them, generation followed generation. The smallest true stars shone on after the great ones disappeared. Their light lasted for times far longer than the universe has existed today. But even their reserves were finite. Eventually, the bright populations thinned and fresh births became uncommon.

The pair's original limitation had preserved something that the successful stars had spent.

Preservation, however, could have lasted indefinitely without another star being made. The hydrogen had no intention of burning. The two bodies were not waiting for an appointed moment. Their unused fuel was a possibility enclosed in an arrangement that could not use it.

That arrangement was slowly changing.

The brown dwarfs were bound by gravity, but their sideways motion kept them circling instead of falling straight together. As they orbited, they sent extremely faint ripples through spacetime. These gravitational waves carried energy away. The cost of one revolution was tiny. Across immense spans of time, the cost accumulated: the orbit became smaller, the bodies drew closer, and the circling became faster.

By the time the galaxy had grown dark, a change almost invisible during its brilliant ages had become decisive.

The pair approached closely enough to stretch each other. Their outer layers distorted. Material began crossing between them. In this nearly equal pair, the exchange grew unstable; it did not settle into a quiet, lasting transfer. Gas streamed outward, while much of the material fell together into one turbulent body.

For a while, it shone fiercely.

That flash alone would not explain the star at the beginning of our story. A collision can turn motion into heat, and a hot body can shine as it cools. The new brightness could have faded away with the energy of the encounter.

But the merger had changed two things together. It had gathered much of the mass of both brown dwarfs into one body, and it had heated their cold material. Now a hot interior bore a weight that neither separate interior had supported. The conditions for using the old hydrogen had changed.

The swollen body settled and contracted. Its surface radiated away energy. Deep inside, nuclear reactions began supplying enough energy to sustain that loss. Hydrogen became helium, as it had in the stars of the vanished bright ages.

The violence of the merger subsided. Its dazzling light gave way to something much fainter and much more enduring.

We have returned to the small red star.

Its future is long because it spends its fuel slowly. Material circulates through its interior, bringing hydrogen into the regions where it can react. The star need not shine brilliantly to continue shining. Around it, the old remnants keep cooling, and the universe keeps expanding. This one beginning has not reversed their history.

The new star is made from matter almost as old as the darkness around it. What is young is the arrangement.

For immense ages, two bodies had carried fuel they could not use. Their separation preserved it; the eventual loss of that separation made it available. A history that seemed to contain only the fading of old warmth has opened into a future of sustained light.

The galaxy's bright youth is over. The star's long life is beginning.
