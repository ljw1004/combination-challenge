# The Lights That Remain

The old star will outlive the spiral that contains it.

For five billion years it has shone with an orange light. It travels in the outer part of a galaxy, taking hundreds of millions of years to complete an orbit. Other stars brighten and die during those long circuits. This smaller, slower-burning star has billions of years still ahead.

Farther inward, a dark lane crosses the galaxy's light. Within it lies a region of gas hundreds of light-years across, threaded with dust. It contains enough material for thousands of stars, but much of that material is dispersed and restless. For now, the lane is conspicuous for the light it hides.

The orange star and the dark gas move around the same centre. Soon they will take different ways out of this familiar arrangement.

Another spiral is approaching. These two galaxies are bound by gravity, and both still contain abundant gas. Their encounter will take more than a billion years to unfold. Almost none of their stars will strike another star. Yet the great winding shapes, containing so many lights that remain intact, will come apart.

What can a collision destroy if nearly everything bright passes through?

Before the galaxies overlap, the old star's orbit begins to change. The approaching galaxy pulls unevenly across the one that contains it. Whole regions stretch. The old star joins an outward stream, bending away from the disk along with millions of others. The spiral's bright centre recedes behind it.

The star itself continues to shine. The forces rearranging its immense surroundings scarcely disturb the compact sphere held together by its own gravity.

Farther inward, the dark lane is bending too. Its gas streams toward other gas as the two galaxies draw through one another. Here the distances between stars offer no escape: clouds extend through those distances. Gas presses into gas. A shock moves through the dark region, heating and compressing it.

The old star crosses the outskirts of the other galaxy. Its new neighbours are still separated by vast intervals. There is no blaze of impact, no sudden extinguishing. The surrounding lights change their positions slowly, and the star travels on into the lengthening stream.

Behind it, the compressed gas cannot simply continue along its old course. Some of its motion has become heat. That heat must escape before much of the material can draw closer together. Radiation carries energy away. Within the crowded region, cold dense knots begin to contract under their own gravity.

The old star is travelling outward. The gas is gathering inward, into many separate centres.

Millions of years pass. Inside the knots, falling material heats the growing bodies. They already shine, releasing energy from their contraction, although dust conceals much of their light. Later, their cores become hot enough for sustained hydrogen fusion. A new source of energy begins supporting them against further collapse.

The dark region has made stars.

Some are massive and brilliant. Their radiation and outflows begin clearing the gas around them. Much of the original material never becomes a star; it is scattered or heated, ending this particular gathering. The region loses the dark outline by which it could once have been followed. In its place are young lights and thinning remnants of cloud.

Among the new stars are small ones that will shine long after the brightest have died.

The orange star is still moving outward when the galaxies begin to separate after their first passage. Their centres pull apart, with long streams trailing behind them. Across that widening distance, the young stars continue their lives. They have formed while the larger collision is barely under way.

They now respond to the encounter differently from the gas that made them. They, too, can pass among other stars. Each new star is held together by its own gravity while the changing pull of the galaxies redirects its journey.

Far out in the stream, the orange star slows in its outward motion. It has travelled beyond the old spiral's bright extent, but it has not escaped the combined gravity of the pair. Its path curves back.

There will be no return to its former orbit.

The galaxies are returning too. At each passage, their gravity stirs the matter around them, transferring some of the energy of their mutual orbit into other motions. Their centres draw together again. The space between those centres narrows, opens, and narrows further. The old star's returning path is bent by a system still changing as it crosses it.

The stars born in the dark lane are carried through the same disturbance. Some remain close together; others spread into the surrounding population. The gas that once joined them has dispersed. Now their common beginning survives in their ages and their composition, even as their paths diverge.

Eventually, the two centres settle into one larger galaxy. In this encounter, the former spirals have become a broad, rounded body, with faint streams still curving beyond its brighter reaches. The merger has taken more than a billion years. The new clusters have aged. Their most brilliant early stars are already gone.

The orange star remains.

It passes through regions containing stars born in the cloud, then travels outward again. Its orbit carries it through a volume that once belonged to two galaxies. Around it, old and younger stars move in many directions. No pattern separates the survivors of one spiral from the survivors of the other.

The dark lane is gone as thoroughly as the winding arms. Yet some of its material now shines beside the ancient light that passed through the encounter almost untouched.

One has lost its orbit. The other has become a multitude of stars. For billions of years they will continue through this shared expanse, held by a galaxy that did not exist when either journey began.
