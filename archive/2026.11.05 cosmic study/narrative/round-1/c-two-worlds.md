# The World That Didn't Stay

The smaller world is nearly round now.

It has taken millions of years to get this far. Stones struck stones. Some broke apart; some held together. Larger bodies drew the scattered pieces in, and the pieces struck hard enough to melt. Inside the growing world, iron sank through softened rock. Outside, a dark skin formed, cracked, and formed again. There is something almost familiar about its curve against the sunlight. It looks like a place.

Follow it. There is another, larger world nearby, but keep the smaller one in sight.

We call the larger world Earth. We have given the smaller one a name too: Theia. No one saw them together. The Moon's surviving rocks, and the motions of Earth and Moon, suggest that a collision made the pair we know. This is one possible history of that meeting.

For now they are separate. Each travels around the Sun. Each carries the weight of everything that has fallen into it. Where an incoming rock disappears beneath a molten surface, it becomes part of the place it struck. A world is built out of things that have stopped being themselves.

Theia has its own horizons. Stand anywhere on it in imagination and the ground curves away beyond your sight. Walk far enough and you would return to where you began, if there were solid ground all the way, if there were time. This world is smaller than Earth, but it is no pebble waiting to be picked up. It has an inside too deep to reach and a night side turned away from the Sun.

The two paths are changing. Other growing worlds pull at them. Close passages alter their courses. Nothing marks the crossing ahead; there is no line laid across the dark.

Then Earth appears above Theia's horizon.

At first it could be another light. As the distance closes, it becomes a disc, then a vast curved surface. On Earth, Theia is swelling in the sky. The same pull that gathered stones into these worlds now draws the worlds toward each other. There is too much speed for a quiet joining, too much mass for either surface to remain a surface.

Theia strikes.

For an instant there are still two bodies, each deformed around the place where they meet. Then the familiar outlines fail. Rock moves like water. Heat turns more rock into vapour. Material from both worlds tears outward in a bright, spreading mass.

Try to keep Theia in sight now.

There is no edge to follow. Its separate horizon has gone. Much of it joins the battered Earth; some of the wreckage is thrown out around the larger body. The Earth beneath that wreckage has lost its old surface too. The collision has mixed and melted the places that existed a moment before.

For millions of years, arrivals like these had built two worlds. This arrival has made it impossible to count them.

Much of the material lifted above Earth falls back. It strikes the molten planet in another rain of heat. Other fragments escape altogether. But some travel fast enough sideways to keep missing the surface as Earth's pull bends their paths. The wreckage circles.

It has not cooled into a tidy ring of stones. There is vapour, liquid rock, a shifting mass in which pieces gather and are torn apart. Close to Earth, the difference in its pull across a clump can wrench the clump open. Farther out, material can hold together. A larger lump sweeps through smaller ones. Their collisions add heat as well as weight.

Something round begins to emerge.

It cannot restore the vanished world. Theia's old surface cannot be fitted back into place. Earth cannot be put back either. Yet the wreckage has not all vanished into the larger planet. Here is a new body, hot from the violence of coming together, circling above a new Earth.

The Moon.

At first it is much closer than the Moon we know. It has a molten surface and none of the dark patches in which people will find a face. As heat escapes, crystals form. Lighter ones rise and collect into a pale crust. The glow of hot rock fades. Sunlight falls on the cooled surface and is reflected back into space.

The Moon shines now even where it no longer burns.

There is more damage to come. Stones strike the crust; larger bodies excavate enormous basins. Later, lava wells up and spreads across some of their floors. It cools dark. The pale high ground and the broad dark plains make the markings that will one day be visible from a doorstep.

Meanwhile the two bodies continue to change one another. Their pull raises tides, and over immense stretches of time the Moon moves farther out. Earth cools. Its surface is broken, buried, remade. The record of the collision grows harder to find beneath everything that follows.

Billions of years pass before there are doorsteps.

Tonight a woman comes home with a bag of groceries. Her child is trying to carry the bread without crushing it. At the gate, the child stops so suddenly that the woman almost bumps into him.

“Look.”

Above the roofs, the Moon is bright enough to show its dark patches. A cloud is moving past it. The child shifts the bread under one arm and points, although there is nothing else she could mistake for what he means.

The woman has seen it thousands of times. There is frozen food in the bag. Her keys are somewhere under the receipt. She stops beside him anyway.

Under their shoes is one of the worlds that came out of the collision. Above the houses is the other. Between them is the distance that lets both remain.

They stand there until the cloud has passed.
