## t021 — Augustine: both infants need forgiveness, whether ill or well

**Read:** His reason is their shared descent from Adam, not a diagnosis of illness. Neither has chosen a wrong act; the healthy infant is no exception to his inherited-condition claim. This reconstructs Augustine’s account, not a medical or moral judgment on sick children.

## t034 — Aquinas: nature cannot prove Father, Son and Spirit are one

**Read:** The Trinity is the belief that Father, Son and Holy Spirit are one God, not three gods. Aquinas says created things do not demonstrate this belief. The next desk examines what its words mean.

**Attribution:** Attributed paraphrase and editorial explanation; see the named source.

## t051 — Lyon: hands bring forgiveness and God’s Spirit, not water-washing

**Read:** The rite claims forgiveness of sins and reception of the Holy Spirit—God dwelling with the believer. It calls this spiritual baptism rather than washing with water. These are its claims, not events the game verifies.

**Attribution:** Lyon ritual III.22–31; IV.3,18–21,49–53; original paraphrase.

## t070 — Both rites use bodies; their authority claims still differ

**Read:** Lyon says its hands continue Jesus’s first followers’ practice. Aquinas says God appoints baptism’s water as a means of giving grace. Both use bodily actions, but that does not establish the same authority or explain the gift in the same way.

**Attribution:** Attributed paraphrase and editorial explanation; see the named source.

## t082 — Forgiven guilt can leave penalties; indulgences concern those penalties

**Read:** In the account being debated, forgiveness of guilt does not remove every remaining penalty or purification. An indulgence concerns that remainder, not a purchase of God’s forgiveness. Luther disputes what the pope can release.

**Attribution:** Ninety-Five Theses 5–6,21,36; background distinction required by the argument, not endorsement of the seller’s claim.

## t104 — Luther: faith receives the promised forgiveness of sins

**Read:** For Luther the promised benefit is forgiveness of sins, strengthening faith and consoling the believer. Receiving the sacrament’s body and blood is not identical to receiving that benefit through trust.

**Attribution:** Luther, Large Catechism V §§20–29,33–37.

## t106 — Marburg: Christ received by faith; bodily presence remains disputed

**Read:** Spiritual reception means sharing in Christ through faith, not deciding where his body is. Article 15 affirms that reception and the supper’s strengthening of faith, while recording disagreement about bodily presence in bread and wine. It supplies no shared physical mechanism.

**Attribution:** Marburg Articles, article 15; “through faith” is a plain-language gloss read with articles 5–8, not a quoted common mechanism.

## t117 — Trent: received goodness grows through later grace-enabled actions

**Read:** For the council, righteousness includes inward renewal: being made just, not only being forgiven. It says the justice already received grows through grace-enabled good actions. Its claim is not merely that actions display an unchanged condition.

**Attribution:** Trent, Session VI, chapters VII and X, canon 24; original paraphrase.

## t118 — Luther: later works do not make me more righteous before God

**Read:** Luther compares a bishop’s work with prior consecration: doing the work does not make the bishop more consecrated. Likewise, works do not make a Christian more holy before God; faith alone does. This denies a role for works in increasing that holiness, not their usefulness.

**Attribution:** Christian Liberty (1520), bodily-discipline discussion and bishop comparison; public-domain text, original paraphrase. “Before God” marks the stated domain, not a denial of practical improvement.

## t123 — Dispute: do later works increase the justice already received?

**Read:** Luther allows practice and service but denies that works make the already-Christian person more holy before God. Trent gives grace-enabled works a role in increasing received justice. We compare these specific texts, not a slogan that one side cares about conduct and the other does not.

**Attribution:** Attributed paraphrase and editorial explanation; see the named source.

## t154 — Luther: the pope controls church-imposed penalties, not God’s mercy

**Read:** In 1517 Luther limits the pope’s own power to penalties imposed by church authority. Declaring God’s forgiveness is not the same as controlling it. This is Luther’s contested account, not a neutral definition of every Catholic position.

**Attribution:** Luther, Ninety-Five Theses 5–7,21,27–28.
