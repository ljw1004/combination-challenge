# Construction research for the builder story

Research date: 6 November 2026. Audience assumption: a curious child around ten. This is input to an original story, not an evaluation of a manuscript or a tested set of home-build instructions.

The worthwhile hook is making toys that actually do something: catch a marble, animate a creature, draw a surprising pattern, reveal a hidden view, or drive where you tell them. Their mechanisms also offer useful understanding beyond the game. Follow the [project philosophy](../../README.md#philosophy) and [physical authoring guidance](../../docs/story-authoring.md#complete-operations-and-elapsed-time): retain the important apparatus and causal steps, while bundling routine cutting, fastening and waiting. A screw does not need its own combination.

The eleven project sketches below propose six to ten substantial operations each. These are original construction outlines, not transcriptions of the linked activities. Sources establish mechanisms and educational precedents; they do not establish that these particular designs work, that a ten-year-old can build them independently, or that the eventual game is enjoyable. Dimensions, tolerances, exact components and assembly access need a physical prototype before any claim of reproducibility. Test actions should visibly operate the toy or improve it; do not manufacture an inventory of “test results” to meet a recipe quota.

## 1. A cardboard creature that reaches across the table

**Worth making:** a long-necked paper creature whose neck stretches when its handles close, able to nose a light paper ball into a goal.

**Seven operations:** cut sturdy link strips; make loose central pivots; connect the crossed links; add handles; attach a lightweight head to the far end; fit feet or a resting stand; adjust a binding pivot while reaching for the ball.

**Physical prerequisites:** card, split-pin fasteners, hole punch, tape and an adequately stiff handle. Links need clearance to rotate; the decorative head must not bridge two joints that need to move independently. [New York Hall of Science’s linkage tutorial](https://nysci.org/pdf/Maker-Tutorial-Linkages.pdf) documents cardboard links and brass pivots as workable construction materials.

**Teach/test:** connected pivots transmit motion; small handle travel can produce greater reach. Compare a short chain with a longer one and feel the loss of stiffness. A joint fastened too tightly binds; a head that is too heavy bends the neck. Avoid depicting a glued crossing as a rotating joint, or implying that increased reach also gives greater pushing force.

## 2. A marble pinball garden

**Worth making:** a replayable tabletop game, with its own original path and targets.

**Eight operations:** stiffen a shallow tray; raise its back on a prop; fit side rails and a return pocket; mount pivoting flippers; connect their handles; make an elastic marble launcher; arrange bumpers and target gates; play and reposition one bumper to repair an unreachable target.

**Physical prerequisites:** smooth card, marble, pivot fasteners, stiff flipper arms, elastic and firmly anchored posts. The ball must have room to pass, the launcher must align with its lane, and the flippers need sweep clearance. [DiscoverE’s pinball activity](https://discovere.org/engineering-activities/build-a-pinball-game/) supplies an educational precedent for building and testing an inclined cardboard playfield in stages.

**Teach/test:** gravity, elastic energy, collisions and lever action. Too little slope stalls the ball; too much makes the game difficult to control. Test a shot before enclosing its route. Avoid weightless marbles running uphill indefinitely, unmoving flippers launching balls, or decorative rails that the actual ball could pass through. Surface friction can help a sliding ball begin rotating; it is not simply an enemy to eliminate.

## 3. A crank-operated bowing bird

**Worth making:** a small mechanical performance: each turn makes a bird bow towards a visitor.

**Eight operations:** brace an open frame; install axle bearings; mount an eccentric cam; fit the crank and axle stops; guide a sliding follower; attach the bird through a pivoted lever; add sufficient return weight; turn and adjust the cam position for a clean bow.

**Physical prerequisites:** stiff card or thin wood, dowel, straw bearings, a smooth cam, follower, pivot and light weight. The cam is attached to its shaft; the shaft rotates inside its bearings. The follower needs gravity or another explicit return force. [Exploratorium’s automata guide](https://www.exploratorium.edu/sites/default/files/tinkering/files/Instructions/cardboard_automata_guide_final_screen.pdf) identifies frame distortion, wandering shafts, inadequate follower weight and missing guides as real failure points.

**Teach/test:** rotation can produce repeated up/down or rocking motion; cam shape and placement change the movement. A perfectly centred circular cam cannot produce vertical lift merely by spinning. Avoid a follower floating above the cam, a shaft glued to its bearing, or an unconnected bird moving in sympathy. The proposed bird and linkage still require prototyping.

## 4. A geared miniature carousel

**Worth making:** a slowly turning stage of the player’s own miniature figures, with a fast hand crank.

**Eight operations:** make a rigid base; install two parallel shaft supports; fit a supplied small driver gear; mesh a matching larger driven gear; attach the carousel platform to the output shaft; fit the input crank; add balanced figures; test and adjust the shaft spacing.

**Physical prerequisites:** a matched pair of ready-made gears, axles, bearings and spacers. Paper and scissors do not manufacture reliable precision plastic gears. Keep both spur gears in the same plane; the crank must turn the actual input shaft rather than imply an absent right-angle transmission.

**Teach/test:** a small driver turning a larger gear trades speed for torque, and directly meshed external gears turn opposite ways. Count marked turns and compare how difficult it is to turn the loaded platform. Too-tight meshing binds; too-wide spacing skips. These relationships are explained in [TeachEngineering’s gear lesson](https://www.teachengineering.org/lessons/umo_challenges_lesson02). Do not claim gears create energy or simultaneously multiply both speed and torque. Changing the characters is artistic work; changing the gear pair changes the mechanism.

## 5. A dock crane for small parcels

**Worth making:** a toy crane that loads lightweight parcels into a model boat or the later rover.

**Eight operations:** brace the tower; fit its boom; install a fixed guide pulley; attach a second pulley to the lifting hook; anchor one rope end to the boom; thread the rope under the moving pulley and over the fixed one; secure the base against tipping; lift a parcel and compare rope travel with load travel.

**Physical prerequisites:** free-running grooved wheels, axles, cord, a light hook and a broad or weighted base. Show the rope’s complete route and its anchored end. The moving pulley travels with the load.

**Teach/test:** the fixed pulley redirects the pull; two supporting rope segments can reduce the required effort, with greater rope travel. Real friction reduces the ideal advantage. [TeachEngineering’s simple-machines lesson](https://www.teachengineering.org/lessons/cub_simple_lesson01) explains direction changes and pulley systems. Test a single-pulley route against the proposed two-pulley route. Avoid granting force multiplication to one fixed pulley, drawing loose rope lifting a load, or ignoring a crane’s tendency to topple. Keep loads at toy scale.

## 6. A creature that opens its mouth when you squeeze

**Worth making:** a remotely operated cardboard creature with a wide, expressive jaw.

**Eight operations:** fold its rigid body; hinge the jaw; mount a needleless output syringe under it; place a lifting pad on the plunger; attach tubing to the hand syringe; connect that tube to the output syringe; add a light return band and secure the tubing; squeeze, release and correct a leaking connection.

**Physical prerequisites:** two smooth-moving syringes, snug flexible tubing, an airtight connection and room for both plungers to travel. The barrel must be supported. A plunger pushing a pad beneath the jaw avoids pretending a straight-moving piston can follow a rigidly pinned circular arc.

**Teach/test:** air transmits force and compresses, producing a springy response. An open tube cannot hold operating pressure. [The Institute of Physics’ syringe investigation](https://spark.iop.org/feeling-springiness-air) explains air’s compressibility; [Museo Nazionale della Scienza e della Tecnologia’s pneumatic pop-up activity, page 28](https://www.museoscienza.it/tinkerlib/download/Activity_Kit_TinkerLib_EN.pdf#page=28) documents syringe circuits animating paper mechanisms. Compare a loose connection with a sealed one. Avoid calling the air-filled apparatus hydraulic, claiming exact equal plunger travel under every load, or depicting air as an inexhaustible power source.

## 7. A periscope for a cardboard lookout

**Worth making:** seeing over the wall of the player’s own miniature fort.

**Six operations:** fold the lower tube with an eye opening; fit its angled mirror bracket; mount the lower mirror; build and join the upper tube; install the upper mirror and viewing opening; aim over a wall and adjust an obstructed light path.

**Physical prerequisites:** two flat craft mirrors large enough for the tube, stable brackets and an unobstructed interior. In the conventional forward-looking arrangement, the mirror planes are parallel and tilted 45 degrees to the tube axis, with reflective faces directed into the required light path. [Science World’s periscope activity](https://www.scienceworld.ca/resource/periscopes/) explains the two reflections.

**Teach/test:** trace light from the object to the upper mirror, lower mirror and eye. Tilt one mirror to see the view disappear; recover it by aligning the path. Avoid drawing rays leaving the observer’s eyes, mirrors reflecting through their backs, or a simple two-mirror periscope magnifying distant objects. Mirror alignment changes what can be seen; ornamental details do not repair an obstructed opening.

## 8. A sliding-bridge tabletop string instrument

**Worth making:** an instrument on which the player can find and repeat a short tune.

**Seven operations:** reinforce a small soundbox; make a raised fixed bridge; add a sliding bridge; fit secure string anchors; tension several strings or elastic loops; make a plectrum; move the bridge and tune a short phrase by listening.

**Physical prerequisites:** rigid enough anchors, free vibrating lengths clear of the box, and a bridge that makes physical contact with the soundboard. Use modest tension and supplied string or bands; the box must survive it. [Pacific Science Center’s box-guitar activity](https://pacificsciencecenter.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/05/cah-rubberband-guitar.pdf) supports the soundbox and bridge approach; [Exploratorium’s Head Harp](https://www.exploratorium.edu/snacks/head-harp) explains the effects of string length and tension.

**Teach/test:** vibration makes sound; shortening a string at approximately constant tension raises its pitch. Compare plucking harder with shortening the vibrating span: loudness and pitch are different. If the string touches the box along its length it is damped. Avoid claiming every thicker elastic band must sound lower regardless of its tension and length, or that pulling an elastic band changes only one physical variable.

## 9. A little lantern with a door switch

**Worth making:** a paper building whose light comes on when its door closes, or a portable lantern with the same switch.

**Eight operations:** fold the housing; fit a translucent window; mount an AA battery holder; lay separate outgoing and return conductors; install a matched LED and series resistor; make a foil door contact across a gap; insulate exposed crossings; close the door and repair a poor contact.

**Physical prerequisites:** low-voltage batteries, conductors, an LED of known polarity, current limiting matched to the supply and an accessible switch. [Exploratorium’s paper-circuit activity](https://www.exploratorium.edu/tinkering/projects/paper-circuits) demonstrates switches and polarity; [Adafruit’s LED introduction](https://learn.adafruit.com/all-about-leds?view=all) explains current limiting. A supplied LED module can contain the resistor, provided the story names that capability.

**Teach/test:** a complete conducting loop powers the light; the switch opens or closes that loop. An LED is directional. Distinguish an open contact from a reversed LED. Avoid a single unexplained wire, two foil paths touching each other, or copying a coin-cell demonstration onto a higher-current AA supply without current limiting. The resistor is functional, not decoration.

## 10. A scribbling beetle

**Worth making:** a three-legged drawing machine that leaves its own strange trails.

**Eight operations:** make a light body; fit three marker legs; mount a low-voltage motor; attach a small eccentric weight; secure a battery holder; wire a switch; provide clearance around the moving weight; run it on paper and adjust one leg or the weight to change the drawing.

**Physical prerequisites:** a matched battery and motor, a secure off-centre mass, a body that transmits vibration, and stable pen contact. [Exploratorium’s scribbling-machine guide](https://www.exploratorium.edu/sites/default/files/pdfs/scribbling_machines.pdf) documents offset motors, clearance, pen placement and weight changes as the mechanism and variables.

**Teach/test:** the rotating unbalanced mass shakes the body; friction and geometry turn that shaking into movement. Compare an initially balanced rotor with an offset one. If the motor spins but the drawing barely moves, investigate the mass and feet; if it stops, inspect clearance and power. Avoid claiming a plain motor magically walks, or promising that a vibration robot can draw an exact chosen picture. Pattern discovery is the payoff. Keep the rotating mass small and retained.

## 11. A radio-controlled delivery rover

**Worth making:** driving a small parcel around an obstacle course, with a clear stop command.

**Ten operations:** stiffen the chassis; mount two independent wheel servos; attach their wheels; add a caster; secure the motor battery pack and switch; install the controller with suitable servo connections; connect signal and common ground; prepare a second controller as the radio handset; assign matching radio groups and motion commands; test forward, turn and stop, then correct wheel direction or trim.

**Physical prerequisites:** supplied continuous-rotation servos, matched wheel hubs, battery packs, two ready-made radio/controller boards and an explicit program. The proposed form uses servos containing their own drive electronics; bare DC motors would instead require a suitable motor driver. The user builds a vehicle, not semiconductor chips. [Micro:bit’s servo guidance](https://support.microbit.org/support/solutions/articles/19000101864-using-a-servo-with-the-micro-bit) distinguishes continuous rotation from position control and explains external power and shared ground. [MakeCode’s radio group reference](https://makecode.microbit.org/reference/radio/set-group) establishes the matching-group requirement; its [remote robot example](https://makecode.microbit.org/projects/milk-carton-robot/connect) demonstrates radio commanding a mechanism.

**Teach/test:** information and power have different jobs; differential wheel motion steers. Test with wheels raised before the floor course. Mirrored motors may need opposite command signs to drive forward. Avoid powering two drive motors from a logic pin, giving position servos unlimited wheel rotation, or implying radio supplies propulsion energy. Add an explicit stop on lost commands in the actual controller design.

## Progression and carryover hypotheses

- **Learn through visible motion:** linkage creature → pinball → cam bird. These can establish pivots, clearance, stiffness and a reason to test before closing a housing.
- **Make motion useful:** carousel and crane add ratios and load paths. Reuse a constructed stage or dock; do not deliver an unexplained finished mechanism later.
- **Explore other ways to transmit effects:** pneumatic creature, periscope and instrument add pressure, light and vibration. Each has a satisfying object as its endpoint; they need not become compulsory parts of one enormous machine.
- **Add electrical power and then information:** lantern → scribbling beetle → rover. Reuse the understanding of a complete circuit and switch. If a particular battery pack, motor or controller is physically transferred between toys, the donor loses that capability. A recurring workshop supply can instead provide additional parts explicitly.

For later fantasy, extend a named function while retaining these causal relationships: an invented lightweight material could permit a larger flying toy; an invented sensor could give the rover a new observation. Establish its bounded ability when introduced. A fictional material should not silently supply motors, bearings, energy, steering and radio. A fantastical destination or creature can be the imaginative reward while the toy’s construction remains intelligible.

For an age-ten manuscript, technical words can follow the visible function: “a sliding rod called the follower” or “the smaller gear you turn.” Keep both what stays fixed and what is free to move apparent in names, artwork and combinations. Use prepared holes, adult-prepared cuts and supplied precision parts where appropriate. Maintain toy-scale loads, hand-operated air systems and low-voltage battery circuits; none of these candidates requires explosives, mains electricity or pressure vessels.

No commercial kit instructions, layouts or artwork were copied. The proposed characters, scenes, toy forms and operation groupings remain original hypotheses. Published educational examples support feasibility of the underlying mechanisms, not a verdict on this story’s learning, accessibility or fun.
