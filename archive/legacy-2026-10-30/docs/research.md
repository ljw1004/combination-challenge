# Designer research and falsifiable design commitments

Research pass: 25 October 2026. This is a synthesis of designer arguments and firsthand development accounts, not a validated theory or a prediction of human enjoyment. All claims below are paraphrases. The proposed measurements are our own operational definitions.

## The experience we are trying to create

The target is **feeling inventive under understandable pressure**: notice a concrete problem, predict how an elemental construction will alter it, commit, and see a consequence that either vindicates the plan or suggests an intelligible revision. Curiosity should come from what capabilities imply in a new situation. A visible recipe can still support discovery if its consequences and uses have depth.

The strongest current justification is a combination of three different arguments: mastery makes changing situations legible [1, 8], expression gives players ownership of a solution [2, 9], and interacting tools can create choices even with clearly previewed threats [10]. None establishes that this particular implementation will be enjoyable. A victory obtained through an obvious repeated action would satisfy the objective while contradicting the intended experience.

## What the sources disagree about

- **Learning versus emotional breadth.** Koster and Cook emphasize learning usable models. MDA and Lazzaro give us reason to examine curiosity, expression, relief, and presentation as separate experiences [1–3, 8]. Our grader must allow a strategically legible encounter to remain emotionally flat.
- **Difficulty versus room to invent.** Into the Breach's designer describes satisfaction and creativity surviving slightly easy battles. Foddy proposes that failure and frustration themselves can have aesthetic value [10, 13]. We should compare the character of a failure and the recovery it invites, rather than automatically reward either harder play or higher success rates.
- **An authored insight versus an authored solution.** Ten Bosch and Blow advocate exploring system structure; Barth describes open-ended construction; Teikari describes carefully authored interactions and cutting unwieldy setups [6, 9, 12]. Several solutions are useful evidence of agency, but solution count alone cannot tell us whether there is an interesting idea to discover.
- **Instrumentable behavior versus lived experience.** Cook offers an explicit behavioral model; MDA cautions that components must be assessed through their effects on experience [2, 8]. A replay can show improved prediction. It cannot establish that making that prediction was pleasurable.

## Source ledger

The ledger distinguishes text actually read from a listing or abstract. Interviews are primary evidence for what the interviewed designer said; they are edited and retrospective, rather than experimental proof. No embedded talks were watched during this pass.

### 1. Raph Koster — Game design is simple, actually

- **Date/access:** 3 November 2025; author's article read. [Original](https://www.raphkoster.com/2025/11/03/game-design-is-simple-actually/)
- **Claim:** For systems design, Koster centers mastery and improving prediction. He distinguishes repeating an action from repeating an unchanged problem, and identifies information about available actions and consequences as necessary feedback.
- **Prediction to test:** After using an elemental interaction once, a player should anticipate it in a different configuration. Repeated deployments should address changing circumstances.
- **Limit:** This is a designer's useful framing, not a demonstration that every kind of pleasure reduces to mastery. We do not adopt the article's neuroscience claims as established facts.

### 2. Robin Hunicke, Marc LeBlanc, Robert Zubek — MDA

- **Date/access:** 2004; five-page author paper text read. [Original PDF](https://www.cs.northwestern.edu/~hunicke/MDA.pdf)
- **Claim:** Mechanics, runtime dynamics, and emotional responses are related but different levels. The authors advocate experience-driven iteration and a vocabulary beyond the undifferentiated label of fun. They explicitly reject a universal formula.
- **Prediction to test:** A rules change intended to create anticipation must produce a recognizable change in decisions or pacing, and eventually in player reports. More recipes alone would not satisfy it.
- **Limit:** A descriptive and design framework does not validate our measurements or supply weights for combining them.

### 3. Nicole Lazzaro — The 4 Keys 2 Fun

- **Date/access:** Undated author summary; it identifies the original presentation as GDC 2004. Summary read; the underlying study was not reanalyzed. [Original](https://www.nicolelazzaro.com/the4-keys-to-fun/)
- **Claim:** Lazzaro distinguishes triumph through adversity, curiosity, changes of feeling, and social amusement as important pleasures.
- **Prediction to test:** A generous-pressure variant may support curiosity even when it reduces adversity; ask about both rather than infer enjoyment from difficulty.
- **Limit:** The short page gives limited methodological detail and promotes consulting services. Its claims about successful games needing multiple keys are not a requirement for this prototype.

### 4. Sid Meier — Interesting Decisions

- **Date/access:** GDC 2012. Official listing/abstract recovered in search results; opening the URL redirected to GDC maintenance. Talk not watched. [Official listing](https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1015756/Do-Say-The-Right-Thing)
- **Claim supported here:** The abstract proposes examining decision categories, pacing, information, and feedback through the lens of interesting decisions.
- **Prediction to test:** Inspect a choice between immediate relief and developing a capability, including what information makes either option defensible.
- **Limit:** We cannot attribute detailed prescriptions from the talk to an abstract. The unusual URL slug is the supplied official listing, not evidence that we watched another session.

### 5. Soren Johnson — Sid's Rules

- **Date/access:** Published January 2009; author repost dated 1 May 2009 read. Johnson reports his experience working at Firaxis. [Original](https://www.designer-notes.com/game-developer-column-5-sids-rules/)
- **Claim:** Large prototype changes can reveal design territory faster than tiny adjustments. His account of Covert Action cautions that individually appealing subsystems can compete for attention.
- **Prediction to test:** Compare an entire discovery action with immediate invention-and-placement. Keep elemental invention connected to the same world process the player is trying to affect.
- **Limit:** Practitioner advice about productive iteration does not establish which change is right. Multiple simultaneous changes require an exploratory, rather than causal, conclusion.

### 6. Marc ten Bosch and Jonathan Blow — Designing to Reveal the Nature of the Universe

- **Date/access:** November 2011 author post about the October IndieCade talk. Author summary read; embedded talk not watched. [Original](https://marctenbosch.com/news/2011/11/designing-to-reveal-the-nature-of-the-universe/)
- **Claim:** Their proposed aesthetic seeks the structure and consequences of interactive systems, distinguishing this from accumulating combinatorial content.
- **Prediction to test:** One consistent property should support a useful inference in more than one setting. A concept whose only contribution is another recipe node fails this test.
- **Limit:** This is an aesthetic position, not an empirical law or a claim that revealing all recipes removes every useful discovery.

### 7. Experimental Gameplay Project — How to Prototype a Game in Under 7 Days

- **Date/access:** 2005 project account; migrated page does not expose a reliable precise publication date. Team account read. The brief identifies contributors Kyle Gabler, Kyle Gray, Matt Kucic, and Shalin Shodhan. [Original](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/how-to-prototype-a-game-in-under-7-days)
- **Claim:** Small prototypes exposed weak ideas quickly. The team emphasizes a usable core, emotional targets, and ownership; it warns that experimental mechanics can still be simple to understand.
- **Prediction to test:** A cheap simulation should expose the main choice before content expansion. Confusing controls or elaborate supporting rules are reasons to simplify.
- **Limit:** Firsthand retrospective from one team, with selection and hindsight effects; their strongest generalizations are not universal results.

### 8. Daniel Cook — The Chemistry of Game Design

- **Date/access:** 19 July 2007; author essay read. [Original](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/the-chemistry-of-game-design)
- **Claim:** Cook models learning through action, simulation, feedback, and an updated mental model. He proposes instrumenting acquisition and breakdown of skills, and stresses that players bring different prior skills.
- **Prediction to test:** Ask for a brief expected effect before an action, then compare it with the world response. Transfer to an unseen encounter should be stronger evidence than a repeated solution.
- **Limit:** Instrumentation detects behavior consistent with a model, not subjective pleasure. The essay's broad psychological and neurochemical claims are not independently verified here and are unnecessary for our harness.

### 9. Zach Barth — SpaceChem postmortem

- **Date/access:** 13 June 2012; designer's firsthand postmortem read. [Original](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/postmortem-zachtronics-industries-i-spacechem-i-)
- **Claim:** Tools, a clear endpoint, and room to construct produced open-ended solutions and ownership. The team removed logically duplicate puzzles. Competing optimization metrics supported self-set improvement goals.
- **Prediction to test:** Find two solutions with different commitments, then test whether the next encounter changes their usefulness. An optional improvement goal may invite another approach after a first win.
- **Limit:** Player reactions are the developer's reported observations. Importing cycle counts or assembly complexity would conflict with this project's permanent-concept premise.

### 10. Justin Ma — Into the Breach designer interview

- **Date/access:** 23 February 2018, Joel Couture interview; Ma's answers read. [Original interview](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/road-to-the-igf-subset-games-i-into-the-breach-i-)
- **Claim:** Clear rules and telegraphed attacks sought understandable failure. Varied priorities and weapons with several uses encourage adaptation. Ma says solving battles can remain satisfying when difficulty is slightly generous.
- **Prediction to test:** Exact next-turn information should coexist with choosing different interventions as the situation changes. Reduced pressure should be tested for additional invention, not dismissed as easier.
- **Limit:** An account of that game's intentions and experience is not proof that our recipe system inherits its strengths. Its tactical richness comes from interactions, not telegraphs alone.

### 11. Zach Barth — Educational games and tutorial design interview

- **Date/access:** 5 May 2013, Gabriel Recchia transcript of an informal conversation; text read. [Original interview](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/spacechem-s-zach-barth-on-educational-games-tutorial-design)
- **Claim:** Barth criticizes SpaceChem's tutorial, difficulty, length, and insufficient playtesting. He describes constant observation and warns against assuming that provided text is read and understood.
- **Prediction to test:** A fresh player should complete the first interaction using the intended onboarding. A capable agent reading a complete rules packet cannot validate visual onboarding.
- **Limit:** This is a candid retrospective, including interpretations of other research. We do not generalize its anecdotal educational outcomes or treat agent reading skill as representative of newcomers.

### 12. Arvi Teikari — Designing Baba Is You's rule-writing system

- **Date/access:** 8 May 2019, John Harris interview; designer's edited answers read. [Original interview](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/designing-i-baba-is-you-i-s-delightfully-innovative-rule-writing-system)
- **Claim:** Some words grew from intriguing or amusing effects; tester alternatives informed variant levels. Excessively specific solutions and too many supporting rules caused levels to be cut.
- **Prediction to test:** Reject recipes that need several exceptions to explain. Use an alternative player solution as a starting point for a new situation that tests its underlying idea.
- **Limit:** Baba's authored puzzles sometimes deliberately preserve a specific insight. Our asymmetric challenge needs several useful plans; copying that constraint literally could recreate a concealed winning chain.

### 13. Bennett Foddy — Getting Over It designer interview

- **Date/access:** 31 January 2018, Joel Couture interview; designer's answers read. [Original interview](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/road-to-the-igf-bennett-foddy-s-i-getting-over-it-i-)
- **Claim:** Foddy explicitly presents enjoyment of failure as a hunch. His level design explores near-victory turning into lost progress, and narration communicates an authorial relationship.
- **Prediction to test:** Keep a failed plan's specific emotional and tactical sequence available for inspection. Ask whether a reversal invited a new attempt or just imposed work.
- **Limit:** His intentionally severe aesthetic is a counterexample to automatic frustration minimization, not a mandate to erase progress or make this prototype punishing.

### 14. Maddy Thorson — Celeste & Forgiveness

- **Date/access:** Undated author article reprinting a short thread; article text and described examples read. [Original](https://www.maddymakesgames.com/articles/celeste_and_forgiveness/index.html)
- **Claim:** Coyote time, buffered jumps, and corner corrections accommodate intended movement, including demanding moves. Difficult tasks can use generous input interpretation.
- **Prediction to test:** Separate mistakes about strategy from mistakes about which interface action will commit. Selection, preview, cancellation, and recovery should make experimentation dependable.
- **Limit:** These are particular platforming techniques. Their relevance here is an analogy about intent and friction, not evidence that undo automatically improves a turn-based game's stakes.

## Turning research into a closed-loop grader

The grader should return a **profile with trace evidence and counterevidence**. It should make a revision recommendation; it should not claim to measure enjoyment on a universal scale. The following are project hypotheses derived from the readings, not dimensions validated by those authors.

| Dimension | Evidence to request | Evidence against the intended experience | Sources informing the question |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Understandable agency | A short expected effect agrees with the subsequent event; failure cause is visible. | Repeated confident errors caused by omitted ordering or an unexplained exception. | 1, 8, 10 |
| Consequential commitment | Branch one state into two defensible actions; identify the different future opportunity each creates. | Many legal actions produce the same result; one action dominates across changing states. | 4, 5, 10 |
| Coherent discovery and transfer | An interaction learned once is correctly used in an unseen arrangement. | Reusing a memorized chain works everywhere, or new effects require arbitrary exceptions. | 1, 6, 8, 12 |
| Ownership and expression | Effective plans differ in their mechanisms and sacrifices. | Cosmetic variety conceals an identical mandatory sequence. | 2, 7, 9 |
| Pacing and recovery | A temporary action creates time for a larger plan; a reversal produces an intelligible adjustment. | Solved threats require identical upkeep; tight pressure removes experimentation. | 3, 10, 13 |
| Interaction friction | Fresh players can select, inspect, cancel, commit, and understand resolution. | The rules packet succeeds while the browser experience remains opaque. | 7, 11, 14 |

For every judgment, require a build/rules version, seed, decision index, observation, action, resulting event, interpretation, confidence, and a rival interpretation. Treat missing evidence as unknown. Counts of wins, novel recipes, and action variety remain diagnostic context. A mechanical test result must not be relabeled an experience finding.

Useful grader challenges include an easy but trivial build, a technically diverse build with one dominant plan, a hard build with unclear failure, and a verbose player who praises an uneventful replay. A grader that prefers these merely for success, count, difficulty, or rhetoric is failing its intended role. Freeze the rubric for a matched comparison; record revisions separately so changing standards cannot manufacture improvement.

## Experiments that could change the design

1. **Discovery cost:** Does spending a turn learning create a useful planning tradeoff, or just delay seeing the invention? Compare separate discovery/deployment against immediate local manifestation. Reject the separate action if it mostly adds dead turns or forces prior knowledge.
2. **Transfer:** Teach one interaction, then change the geometry or threat. Compare a fresh player with an experienced one on the unseen situation; distinguish rule transfer from a repeated sequence.
3. **Pressure and breadth:** Hold the rules constant while allowing more time. Look for a wider set of defensible constructions, visible revision, and fewer repetitive emergency actions. Either variant can win this comparison; a larger success rate alone is insufficient.

Do not treat these as a complete testing agenda. Run the most consequential uncertainty first. If several mechanics change together, describe the result as exploratory.

## Return to the sources after the experiments

After the first playtest batch, return to the original passages that the observations challenge. Keep both the initial interpretation and the revised interpretation in the iteration record. In particular:

- If agents exploit one recipe everywhere, revisit Koster's changing problems, Ma's varied priorities, and Barth's removal of logical duplicates. Adding content is weaker than changing why an existing tool is useful.
- If a technically rich state still produces bookkeeping, revisit the distinction between mechanics and experience in MDA and the simplifying cuts in Teikari's account.
- If removing a discovery turn increases wins but makes choices automatic, revisit the intended commitment rather than declaring convenience an improvement.
- If pressure produces repeated emergency actions, revisit Ma's creative room and Lazzaro's curiosity. If a harsh reversal becomes a reported highlight, revisit Foddy before smoothing it away.
- If agents praise a build that the trace does not support, revisit Cook's observable loop and the limits of our agent population. The response is a more discriminating experiment, not a more persuasive praise prompt.

Human enjoyment, visual readability for newcomers, and a voluntary desire to replay remain unvalidated until people can try the slice. Designer literature gives us reasons to run particular tests and vocabulary for diagnosing them; the closed loop still has to earn each inference.
