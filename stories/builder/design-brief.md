# A Fair in the Air

## Intent

A making story for a curious ten-year-old. Build toys that invite somebody else to have a go: a wind racer, marble game, crank dragon, optical toys, lifting crane, musical machine, pneumatic grabber, animation drum, drawing rover and shadow theatre. Learn what the parts contribute, predict what will happen, test, and make a purposeful adjustment. The toys should be worth building even without the larger plot. The story uses 181 authored combinations across 29 playable chapters and an ending; a legal completion uses 170 combinations, with the remainder supporting alternate experimental orders. The approximate 200-combination target is subordinate to worthwhile work.

The player is a child working in Aunt Jo's garden workshop during the days before the neighbourhood's bring-a-thing fair. Jo is a companion with dry humour, not an instruction dispenser. Short dialogue and concrete happenings carry the story. The player gets to do the interesting work. No grades, badges, generic praise or pretend scarcity.

## Arc and hook

The invitation says: **Bring something that makes somebody ask, “Can I have a go?”** The early creations are small and individually delightful. Their shared mounting holes and usefulness outside the bench are practical details, not a mystery vocabulary. Jo has a battered unfinished drawing headed “A fair in the air”; its apparent impossibility becomes understandable near the end.

After the real mechanisms are established, Jo brings out an experimental **lift tile**. This is the one fictional technology: a powered tile provides a limited upward force, carries no person, and stops lifting when its current is interrupted. It needs an ordinary battery, support, balance and control. It does not supply energy or fix other mechanisms. Testing reveals limits through observable outcomes. All motors, gearing, optics, steering, circuits and structures remain recognizable real mechanisms.

The final invention is a tethered, approximately metre-long flying whale carrying the original crank dragon and star projector above the garden. The rover draws the welcome banner on the ground; the other toys remain garden stalls. Earlier completed objects return in their original identities and contribute actual functions, without being silently miniaturized. The tile applies upward force above the suspended deck and low battery; three separated garden tethers constrain drift and turning at full height. Jo guides the handling lines while the player lowers current for a landing. Emergency power cutoff is tested only over the low padded cradle and is distinct from controlled descent. The ending earns a change of scene, an invitation to play, and a view of what the player made.

The whimsical reward is a machine that could only fly in this computer game, assembled from enough honest mechanisms that the player can explain most of it. Fiction begins explicitly with the lift tile, not with covertly impossible early toys.

## Chapter progression

1–2. Pinwheel and wind racer: visible movement, wind direction and a race to the watering can.
3–4. Marble pinball: slope, a recoverable pocket, a launcher, flippers and a playable stall.
5–6. Crank dragon: eccentric cam, guided follower, spring contact and two different waves.
7–8. Periscope and kaleidoscope: two reflections over a barrier, then repeated reflections of three coloured pieces.
9–10. Lifting crane: bracing, winch and rope, supported low-load tipping, ballast and ratchet.
11–12. String-wave machine: motor and belt, tension, visible modes and sound.
13–14. Pneumatic grabber: rigid jaw links, needleless syringes, air, compliant pads and a fruit game.
15–16. Animation drum: a sequence of fish drawings, slits, rotation and a shared picture show.
17–18. Drawing rover: independently fitted wheel/drive modules, remote control, wiring, trim and deliberately imperfect lettering.
19–20. Star projector: aperture, lamp, lens, focus and a slowly turning punched disc.
21–22. Fictional lift tile: load, support, power control and balance; the crane helps move the finished module.
23–24. Whale frame and skin: real load paths, accessible service opening and unchanged lift module with supplied extensions.
25–26. Original attractions and drive: mounted dragon/projector, two speeds, retained couplings and a clear guard.
27–28. Low supported flight and garden preparation: trim, separate controls, stop, cradle, screen and tethers.
29. The guests arrive: the rover draws a banner; the whale rises and its moving stars reach the high screen. Kit asks for a turn.
30. Ending: the invitation has become an invitation from the player to somebody else.

Variation comes from assembly, observation, consequential adjustment and use. Routine repeated fittings are grouped. The combined final flight belongs to the guests; the earlier ten-centimetre test has a distinct mechanical purpose.

## Authoring and physical rules

- Use short chapters with about 4–8 initial selectable objects where practical, never more than ten. Subassemblies, fitting and testing should vary the action. Do not pad chains with fastening each identical screw separately.
- Assembly states normally form a fixed hotspot on the work surface. A part added to it is incorporated; the successor has all previous parts plus the actual addition. Final tested toys can become movable when lifted out of the jig, allowing later use.
- A fixed item's successor is fixed in the same chapter until deliberately released. Never require dragging a fixed item. A later chapter explicitly re-presents any fixed object it needs. Physical tools remain tools; incorporated material is consumed. Avoid story-wide reusable process tokens when a real tool or testing context says what happens.
- Use goal wording to state a purpose, not recite a complete recipe. Result text makes the effect perceptible; selected results add a useful explanatory sentence. Do not make every attachment open a paragraph.
- Every supplied pre-made component states its included hardware when relevant. A motor, battery holder or receiver may be an ordinary ready-made module; no unexplained drive, bearings, electrical supply or fasteners appear halfway through.
- No human riders, mains electricity, pressurized launchers, exposed high-speed blades or hazardous construction instructions. Ordinary compatible low-voltage modules, enclosed mechanisms and prepared parts suit the fiction.
- Finished toys should have a use scene. These can include meaningful operation, adjustment or play instead of a passive congratulation.
- Names and descriptions are original. Research general mechanisms from primary educational sources. No copied kit text, page compositions, branding or characters.

## Mechanical models and visual direction

The user identified physical authenticity and range of motion as the central artwork risk and suggested the installed Blender. Use canonical dimensioned 3D assemblies as the source of truth for all mechanically connected parts. Their shared geometry supplies loose-part views, partial assemblies, completed toys and later installed appearances. Metres and a consistent world coordinate convention govern dimensions; presentation may vary camera framing without silently resizing fitted parts. The retained authoring models and rendering scripts document the production method.

Prototype difficult mechanics before final art production. Model and review motion through the full range, including cam contact, follower travel, link lengths, pivot clearance, wing sweep, flipper travel, pulley/rope paths, syringe travel and jaw closure. Use fresh reviewers with the actual geometry and several viewpoints; distinguish analytic constraints, sampled clearance checks and visual inspection from physical validation of a manufactured toy. Never conceal a rigid-part deformation or an unmodelled actuator with an attractive still image. Source models, motion definitions and selected production renders are retained authoring deliverables; raw verification output remains scratch.

This release uses still renders. Animation belongs to a separate phase; direct rendering of the simple models is a possible future approach. Retain simple reusable geometry and motion controls; do not add video, animated images or a new rendering engine in this phase.

Original workshop diagrams rendered from the shared models: clear cut-paper colour, dark blue contour, warm birch/cardboard surfaces, teal mechanisms, orange moving parts, visible holes and fasteners, open negative space. Orthographic or shallow three-quarter views must make joins legible. Non-uniform dimensions represent the part's relationship to its assembly; a washer must not appear the size of a chassis. Fixed construction states share one family projection and canvas, so an added part does not make the rest of the toy jump. Loose small fitting groups use a 44 CSS-pixel readability floor; fitted parts keep their physical dimensions. Literal grabber test objects receive selectable padding, not enlarged drawings. All four flat animation-strip stages keep one scale; the blank and written invitation banner likewise share one crop and scale. See the standing item-artwork and icon guidance for the rendering rules.

The projector's three loose disc stages share a near-normal inspection view at 1.3125 times the fixed assembly projection, making the real perforations legible. The loose lens and lens-holder inspections use 4.2× and 3.5× projections respectively, alongside the ordinary loose-fitting readability floor. Their fitted dimensions remain unchanged. Its actual removable lid rests inside-up beside the box during construction, then closes before testing. The fitted baffle honestly obscures most of the disc face; the loose view supplies that detail.

Celebrations use a more expansive illustrated garden at changing times of day, while preserving the toy's shape, materials, colours and fitted parts. Backgrounds carry atmosphere and setting; interactable drawings are self-contained and do not depend on invisible seams into painted scenery. The periscope and kaleidoscope rewards include actual rendered eye views through their mirrors. The projector's stars use its actual punched mask and an explicit ideal thin-lens diagram on the receiving cloth, without implying a photometric measurement. No parts-list panel: the available game objects already serve that purpose.

## Evaluation

Apply the standing authoring and evaluation guides. Fresh informed reviewers assess necessity, engineering and narrative; source-unexposed players use the actual CLI interfaces. Begin with a full `--fast` screen, revise, repeat affected passages with new players, and conduct a full `--detailed` screen plus multi-player checks of the risky passages. Explicit one-player screening designs allow broad iteration; final selected passages use three independent players and independent grading/synthesis. AI evidence does not establish enjoyment by children.

Falsifiers include: a project with no reason to make it outside this plot; more attention to named intermediate states than useful principles; a late fictional component that makes earlier learning irrelevant; players repeatedly finding a sequence prescribed and unrewarding; or assembly artwork that silently changes or adds unrelated parts.

The live manuscript and runtime artwork belong in `stories/builder`. This is the single active brief for that story and its later study iterations. Retained development sources, research and evaluation outcomes document how the accepted work was reached; they do not replace this intent.

The low-flight chapter reserves its full diagram canvas for labeled views of the actual nose dip and open service hatch. These are additional cameras on the same unchanged assembly, not substitute drawings. The final-flight chapter likewise reserves the full canvas for the whale’s rise. Assembly chapters and garden preparation use receiving bounds around the visible workpiece, keeping loose supplies clear of goals while preserving the full artwork and its registration.
