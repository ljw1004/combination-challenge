# E — What can come apart without breaking?

Fresh expansion candidate, 6 November 2026. Written against the current design brief and Acts I–III; no manuscript changes. This is an authored direction, not a review of the other candidates.

**Offer: 12 complete operations in four short scenes.** The worthwhile subject is smaller than the requested 30–45-operation expansion. A compact stellar encounter can destroy a pair, remove a moon, send a small world into interstellar space, exchange material between suns, or exceptionally merge two stars. Those are substantial additions. I cannot make thirty distinct operations from them without repeating the existing gas aftermath, splitting encounters into orbital adjectives, or opening a new survey of unfamiliar stellar remnants. Do not select this candidate on the expectation that it closes the count gap by itself.

## The new story

The galaxy collision has made a dense cluster from gas that once lay in a spiral. Inside it, the spaces between stars are smaller, although overwhelmingly larger than the stars themselves. Most encounters still end without contact. Some end without the original companions.

One star escapes a close stellar pair. Its path carries it past the young planetary system already made in Act II. The main worlds stay around their sun. A small rubble world and an outlying moon do not. A different, slower stellar passage takes a few icy bodies into another star's company. Later, those same pieces shed material in the unfamiliar warmth.

Back in the cluster, one exceptional encounter brings stellar surfaces together. Two stars cease to exist as separate bodies. One larger star remains, and some of their gas spreads outward.

The ending now holds three distinct answers: bodies preserved while their companionship ends; material preserved while its host changes; bodies physically merged while some of their material escapes. The galaxies' shared remnant contains all three histories. The normal late crossing of the actual `lane-born-stars` remains the principal galaxy-scale payoff.

Proposed closing lines for this excursion:

> The little rubble world has lost its sun, not its stones. The outer moon no longer follows the giant. Elsewhere, ice from their old system is shedding dust beside a different star.
>
> Within the cluster, two lights have become one. Almost every other star still keeps its own surface. A collision can undo an arrangement on one scale and leave a smaller arrangement whole.

## Position and continuity

Place the four scenes after **The unfinished system** and before **After the brightest stars**, with the surrounding chronology adjusted only if the selected stellar lifetimes require it. These scenes are late in the player's experience, not a claim that short-lived stars survive until the galaxies finally merge. The tail-base cluster and the original nursery began near one another; the escaping star travels between them while the galaxies separate. Establish that geography in one sentence and in the scene composition, rather than transporting the entire planetary system into the cluster.

The following imports remain unchanged in their later jobs: `ringed-giant`, `icy-survivors`, `rocky-system`/`c-cratered-system`, `lane-born-stars`, and the surviving tail cluster. In particular, the giant is **not** ejected. The reservoir recipes below use different small portions and preserve the reservoir.

The actual unique objects used are `b-rubble-pile`, produced in **What an impact leaves**, and `b-outer-small-moon`, produced in **Too close to remain a moon**. Both are current goal results, and neither has a later recipe use in the inspected acts. Their new paths need a continuity note in the system description: the giant retains its rings and close moons but loses this particular distant moon. Do not say that every component of the former system survives intact. The rubble body's earlier impact history must leave it on a crossing path that reaches this encounter; its weak internal cohesion does **not** make its stellar orbit easier to unbind. Distance, orbital phase and encounter geometry do that work.

The new stellar individuals are selected, disjoint members of the already-created `a-tail-stars` population. They are not extra stars appearing outside its material history. A playable version must make the actual derived cluster a prerequisite for the cluster scenes, and must account for these selected members when describing the later surviving cluster. Merely declaring their names as globally available primitives would be inadequate.

## Scene 1 — The distances inside the lights

Opening:

> The stars made at the tail's base still travel together. Some travel in pairs. A star passing between widely separated companions meets a different arrangement from one passing beside a close pair.

Purposes: discover what a passage can separate; discover what a close pair can send away.

Starting things: the actual tail cluster as the receiving region; a widely spaced pair; a close pair; a second pair with one smaller companion; a passing star; a separate wind-bearing star approaching the close pair; a heavier newcomer approaching the second pair. The pair separations and relative sizes must be visible before their results. No portable Gravity or Energy.

| # | Complete operation | Contribution and actual consequence |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Widely spaced star pair + Passing star → Two stars travelling apart** | The pair supplies an existing weak binding; the passer changes the members' motions differently. Both former companions survive, but no longer circle each other. The passing star also survives and is not copied into the result. |
| 2 | **Wind-bearing cluster star + Close star pair → Star escaping the cluster** | The single star encounters a moving pair and takes away some of the pair's orbital energy. The pair remains bound more tightly; the single star can leave the cluster. The output is the actual incoming single star, not a manufactured extra star. |
| 3 | **Star pair with a smaller companion + Heavier newcomer → Star pair with a new companion** | A selected three-star encounter leaves the newcomer bound to one original member while the smaller former companion departs. The departing member remains in the narration; the one output follows the new bound pair. No star has been fused or destroyed. |

The result of 2 retains a conspicuous identity—a pale blue-white point and its asymmetric wind envelope—which can be recognized in the next scene. Do not create an additional “accelerated star”, “outward star” and “escaped star” ladder. The one encounter is the operation.

For operation 3, use an unmistakable two-point pair icon with the newcomer replacing the smaller point. The result name and picture together must communicate changed membership without introducing the term *exchange interaction* as inventory. This is the least immediately inferable operation in the scene; test it forwards before retaining it.

## Scene 2 — The small things a sun can lose

Opening:

> The escaping star passes through the outskirts of the young system. It misses the sun and its worlds. The loose rubble world and the giant's small outer moon lie farther from the centres that held them.

Purposes: follow the actual small bodies through a close passage; follow a portion of loose debris that is sent inward.

Starting things: the same escaping star, `b-rubble-pile`, `b-outer-small-moon`, `collision-debris`, and the existing host with its inner worlds as the physical background. The giant and close moons remain visible but need no preservation recipe. A second, slower passer enters the final operation's part of the scene; its path and the relevant icy reservoir are shown at the edge of the same system.

| # | Complete operation | Contribution and actual consequence |
|---|---|---|
| 4 | **Small rubble world + Escaping star → Rubble world without a sun** | The actual previously assembled body reaches the outer part of its crossing path; the passing star changes its motion enough for it to escape. Its internal binding and its binding to the host are separate: it remains a small body held together as rubble. “Without a sun” means no longer bound to a star, not suddenly cold or invisible. |
| 5 | **Small outer moon + Escaping star → Former moon circling the sun** | The same stellar passage removes this distant moon from the giant while leaving the selected moon bound to the original host star. It remains a coherent body. This is a different loss of membership from 4, not a second renamed escape. |
| 6 | **Debris between the planets + Escaping star → Stones falling toward the sun** | A separate portion of the extensive debris reservoir is deflected onto star-intersecting paths. Operations 4–6 are views of selected constituents during **one** stellar passage, not three passages by duplicate stars. Preserve the escaping-star identity. The outcome parameters must be jointly possible: an encounter that strips a very distant small moon must not secretly eject the main giant, unbind the close moons, or destroy the inner system. This needs a selected dynamical model before manuscript adoption.

Operation 7 is a separate slow encounter over the cluster's continuing dispersal, not the fast star somehow slowing down. Do not claim that many comets are routinely stolen or that these exact outcomes are typical. The actual ice is old disk material, with a changed host.

## Scene 3 — Different surroundings

Opening:

> The sunward stones meet the star itself. Ice taken by the slower passer begins a different journey around a different light. Beyond both systems, the fast star reaches gas with its own wind still flowing ahead of it.

Purposes: follow solid matter that no longer keeps its former form; follow what the escaping star does to surrounding gas.

Starting things: the sunward stones; the transferred icy population; the escaping star; a portion of `c-cold-neighbour`; the actual young star's outer layers as a fixed physical region; the warm inner passage around the slow star as a second fixed region. The slow star and warm passage refer to the same individual introduced in 7.

| # | Complete operation | Contribution and actual consequence |
|---|---|---|
| 8 | **Sunward stones @ Outer layers of the young star → Rock material mixed into stellar gas** | The stones enter the real star, heat, vaporize and become dispersed among stellar material. They do not make a second star. This finishes the directed fall established by 6; no cooling or elapsed-time ingredient is needed. |
| 9 | **Transferred icy bodies @ Warm passage around the other star → Comets shedding gas and dust beside another sun** | The old ice receives radiation at its new close approach and sublimates; the surviving nuclei and their released material are one physical comet population. This repeats a known material response once, to show that the same ice can acquire another stellar history. The orbit must actually reach the warm region. Capture alone does not guarantee it. |
| 10 | **Escaping star + Cold surrounding gas → Curved gas shock ahead of the star** | The star supplies its wind and its supersonic motion through the gas. The cloud supplies material piled up and heated along the curved front. The star remains distinct and continues travelling. This is a consequence of its escape, not another “star lights cloud” recipe. |

A loose star has become the source of a moving disturbance. The young host has kept its worlds while swallowing some of their original solid ingredients. Another star now carries pieces of the disk it never formed. There is no extra action for observing, naming or recording these histories.

The bow shock is conditional on a sufficiently strong stellar wind and a suitable relative speed and gas density. If the retained chronology makes that star too old or its wind too weak, remove operation 10; do not make the surrounding gas rejuvenate it.

## Scene 4 — When the surfaces meet

Opening:

> Back in the cluster, most passing stars still miss. A close pair has acquired a new companion, and another star approaches. This particular encounter reaches a distance that the others did not.

Purposes: follow the changed pair through actual surface contact; follow some matter it fails to retain.

Starting things: the actual new pair from 3; one further, separately identified cluster star; a nearby portion of the tail's existing gas. The intruder and the pair are the only stellar inputs. No new black hole, neutron star, fusion material, Time or Heat appears.

| # | Complete operation | Contribution and actual consequence |
|---|---|---|
| 11 | **Star pair with a new companion + Close-passing cluster star → One larger star inside expanding gas** | The selected three-star encounter brings the two bound stars into physical contact. Their gas collides, loses orbital energy and mostly coalesces; some material escapes. The third star departs and remains a star. The single output follows the merger remnant and its associated ejecta as one physical system. |
| 12 | **Merger star's expanding gas + Nearby tail gas → Shell carrying material from the merged stars** | The expanding portion sweeps surrounding gas into a shock and shell. The merger star remains where it was; only a specified ejecta portion joins the shell. This gives the rare merger a material consequence beyond a brighter point. |

For operation 12, **Merger star's expanding gas is a component of the one result from 11, not a newly supplied primitive**. In a playable representation, the recipe operand should be the derived composite from 11, preserved while an explicitly described ejecta portion interacts with tail gas. Its display name can retain the star to avoid pretending that the result was only a gas reservoir. The table names the portion to make its contribution clear; it is not permission to bypass the producer.

The operation in 11 bundles the encounter, contact, coalescence and immediate mass loss. Do not split it into “eccentric pair”, “contact pair”, “hot merger”, “settled merger” and “older merger”. Do not claim complete mixing, a newly reset lifetime, or that most cluster passages make stars collide. The one merger is the local exception; the galaxy-scale result still concerns the enormous majority that survive separately.

## Scientific basis and boundaries

These primary sources support the mechanisms, not the statistical likelihood or full joint chronology of this selected fictional sequence.

- [Geller et al., *Consequences of Dynamical Disruption and Mass Segregation for the Binary Frequencies of Star Clusters*](https://arxiv.org/abs/1310.1085) models preferential disruption of wide, weakly bound pairs in a young rich cluster. This supports operation 1; it does not justify treating all wide pairs as equally fragile.
- [McMillan and Hut, *Binary–single-star scattering VI*](https://arxiv.org/abs/astro-ph/9604015), and [Heggie, Hut and McMillan, *Binary–single-star scattering VII*](https://arxiv.org/abs/astro-ph/9604016), support encounters that exchange members, eject stars and sometimes lead to mergers. Operations 2, 3 and 11 are selected outcomes. Their rates and exact outcomes depend on masses, separations and approach geometry.
- [Wang, Perna and Leigh, *Planetary Architectures in Interacting Stellar Environments*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.05727) simulates ejection, transfer and collision outcomes from single/binary stellar encounters. It motivates operations 4 and 6 but does not verify the particular jointly surviving system proposed here.
- [Badoux and Portegies Zwart, *Planet–moon ejections in close stellar encounters*](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.16402v1) directly models different planetary and lunar fates under flybys. It supports distinguishing the binding of a moon to a planet from that of the planet to a star, including moons detached from planets. A geometry preserving the actual giant and close moons while giving operation 5 needs selection; the paper is not blanket validation of this composite system.
- [Hanse et al., *Capture of exocomets and the erosion of the Oort cloud due to stellar encounters in the Galaxy*](https://arxiv.org/abs/1710.05866) simulates transfers between comet reservoirs. Its Galactic-field captures require very slow, close encounters and are rare. This is a boundary on operation 7, not evidence that routine fast flybys steal comets. Applying capture to the younger, more compact reservoir here needs a conditions-specific calculation. [Wang et al., *Giant planet swaps during close stellar encounters*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.08366) independently demonstrates host exchange in other selected three-body configurations; this candidate does not silently substitute a giant planet for its small-body reservoir.
- [Meyer et al., *Models of the circumstellar medium of evolving, massive runaway stars moving through the Galactic plane*](https://arxiv.org/abs/1408.2828) models wind bow shocks and their dependence on stellar wind, speed and surroundings. Operation 10 requires a surviving appropriate star at the chosen age. It is not caused by a bare point of light pushing gas mechanically.
- [Glebbeek et al., *Structure and evolution of high-mass stellar mergers*](https://arxiv.org/abs/1307.2445) follows hydrodynamic collisions and the resulting stellar structures. Merger remnants need not be completely mixed or simply reset to the age of a new star. This supports the conservative remnant description in 11, without a promised mass, colour or remaining lifetime.

## What this candidate can earn

The four scenes offer twelve operations, including two familiar aftermath operations: comet sublimation in 9 and swept gas in 12. Their justification is following material into a changed relationship. If that relationship does not make the repeat worthwhile in fast play, cut them; do not replace them with different adjectives.

The clearest forward-comprehension tests are: a wide pair versus a tight pair meeting a passer; the actual loose rubble world meeting the fast star; the outer moon losing its planet while remaining with the sun; and two stellar surfaces finally making contact. Ask for the anticipated physical event before showing the reading. Exact slingshot, exchange and capture outcomes are less obvious than ordinary collisions, so test the names and drawings independently of scientific plausibility.

This direction is useful as a short, consequential addition or as an alternative to weaker repeated debris scenes. It is **not a credible route from 148 to approximately 200 recipes on its own**. Its distinctive contribution is that what a collision destroys can be a companionship, while every material body remains.
