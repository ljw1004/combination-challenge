# What the Star Could Not Keep

The star had made an interior it could no longer support.

Nothing had been added from outside. Its own substance was changing. Beneath a vast red surface, beneath layers still releasing heat, iron was accumulating at the centre. The star had arrived at this condition by doing what had sustained it for millions of years: bringing small nuclei together to make larger ones.

Why should one more act of making undo the whole star?

Its earlier transformations had paid for themselves. Hydrogen became helium, releasing energy into the crowded gas. That energy helped maintain the pressure which held the star open against its own gravity. When the centre contracted, it grew hotter; fusion quickened, and the greater heat resisted further contraction. The star could correct a disturbance by burning differently.

But every correction used material that could not be used in the same way again.

Helium accumulated where hydrogen had been. Later, under greater heat and pressure, helium nuclei joined to make carbon. Some carbon captured more helium and became oxygen. Each new arrangement changed the conditions under which anything further could happen. The centre had to contract and grow hotter again before its former products could become fuel.

Now oxygen was burning in a turbulent shell around the core. Enormous currents carried fresh material into hotter regions and swept the products away. Below that shell, further reactions were building nuclei near iron. The star was still making heat, still supporting its outer layers, still shining into the surrounding darkness.

The iron core grew.

Iron lay near the end of this source of energy. Its nuclei were so tightly bound that joining them into heavier nuclei would require energy instead of releasing it. More heat could not turn this accumulation into another sustaining fire. The star had made something durable at the expense of its own ability to endure.

For a while, densely crowded electrons supported the core. Then the growing core became too massive for that support. Compression drove electrons into nuclei, weakening the resistance to collapse. The rising heat began to tear nuclei apart, consuming energy where the star needed energy released.

The centre fell inward.

Across the enormous red surface, light continued to escape. The catastrophe was still buried too deeply to affect it. Inside, a core thousands of kilometres across collapsed towards a sphere only tens of kilometres wide. In less than a second, the separation between nuclei was largely abolished. Much of the matter became neutrons, packed to the density of an atomic nucleus.

The innermost collapse halted. A shock ran outward into the material still falling in.

It stalled.

The layers above continued to descend. The shock spent energy breaking apart the nuclei it encountered. Contraction had released an immense store of gravitational energy, but most of that energy was escaping as neutrinos: particles that could pass through almost the entire star without interacting with it. A collapse did not automatically become an explosion. The newly made elements could still be carried inward.

But this star's falling layers were uneven. The great currents in the oxygen shell had left dense streams and less dense regions. Around the collapsed core, matter churned. A small fraction of the passing neutrinos surrendered energy to it. Heated material rose in broad plumes, pushing against the stalled shock.

The shock began to move again.

It crossed the oxygen shell, overtook the outer layers and broke through the red surface. The star's enclosing shape came apart. Material that had pressed inward now rushed outward at thousands of kilometres a second. Carbon, oxygen and silicon escaped among the other products of the interior. Some nuclei were changed again by the shock's brief, ferocious heat.

At the centre remained the compressed core. Around it, the rest of the star expanded beyond recovery.

For a time, the wreckage shone more brightly than the star ever had. Yet the important change was already a loss of heat. Expansion spread the gas through an increasing volume. Radiation carried energy away. The conditions that had made new elements were disappearing, and with them the conditions that had prevented those elements from joining in other ways.

The oxygen had never been stone. The silicon had never been sand. Within the burning interior, their nuclei had travelled among stripped electrons in a gas too hot for lasting chemical structures. A star could make the ingredients of minerals while destroying any mineral brought into the region that made them.

Now the fleeing material cooled.

Electrons settled around nuclei. In denser knots of the expanding gas, atoms met and sometimes remained together. Carbon and oxygen formed molecules. Silicon joined oxygen. Encounters that would once have ended in immediate destruction could leave something intact for the next encounter.

There was a narrowing opportunity. The gas was becoming cool enough to bind, but also so widely dispersed that collisions were becoming rare. In some regions almost nothing assembled. Elsewhere, clusters acquired more atoms before the expanding cloud thinned too far. Chemical bonds held those atoms in solids. The star's former interior began to fill with dust.

Years passed. The dust cooled and grew in the shelter of the denser clumps. It was distributed through a volume enormously greater than the vanished star, but its atoms were held together more intimately than they had been in the luminous gas. They no longer needed the pressure of an entire star to remain neighbours.

Across the glowing remnant, the new grains intercepted visible light. They absorbed it and released the energy at longer wavelengths. Dense patches became dark against the brighter gas beyond. The explosion had spread the star's substance out; the new bonds were giving that substance boundaries again.

At the centre, the compact remnant remained. Around it, carbon and minerals crossed the widening darkness in clouds of solid matter. No planet had formed. Nothing required one to follow.

The star had held itself open by making light. What it could no longer hold was beginning to cast a shadow.
