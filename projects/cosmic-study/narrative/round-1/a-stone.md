# Room for One Stone

Nora was allowed one stone.

Her father said this while carrying the last box to the van. There were seventeen stones on the kitchen floor, arranged from best to worst. One had a white stripe right around it. One looked like a sleeping dog. The best was green when wet, though this was difficult to demonstrate because someone had packed the bowl.

“One,” he said again. “Put the others back.”

Nora carried them outside in the bottom of her jumper. She put the dog beneath the hedge. She put the striped one where she would know to look, though they were moving too far away to come back after school.

By the gate lay a stone she had never seen before. Black, with a corner missing. Inside, it was grey and speckled.

She nearly kicked it into the road.

Before there could be a stone, there had to be something that could become one.

Some of its oxygen was made inside a star so large that our Sun would have looked small beside it. Deep under the star's shining surface, the centres of lighter atoms joined, making heavier ones and releasing heat. That heat helped hold up the weight above. For a while, making new things was what kept the star from falling in.

Then the supply that sustained its centre ran out. The centre collapsed. A shock tore through the layers around it, flinging newly made material into space.

There was no black stone in that explosion. Nothing with a corner, nothing anyone could pick up. There were scattered ingredients, spreading farther apart.

They mixed with material from other dead stars. The iron that would one day show as little bright flecks had its own history. So did the silicon that would join oxygen in the stone's minerals. None of their old stars would be there to light the place where they finally met.

In the cold, atoms gathered into tiny grains. A grain could be broken, heated, made again. Clouds drifted through clouds. Much of the material went elsewhere. Some of it entered a cloud that began to collapse under its own weight.

The middle grew hot. A new sun formed there.

Around it, gas and dust swept in a broad, flat swirl. In that crowded place, some dust was briefly heated until it melted. Little drops rounded themselves, then cooled into beads of stone, smaller than seeds. The atoms inside them were old; these particular beads were new.

One pale bead will still be visible in Nora's stone.

For now it travelled among other beads, scraps of metal and finer dust. Touching sometimes meant sticking. Sometimes a collision pulled apart what earlier collisions had made. The little gatherings grew and broke and grew again.

Where enough material collected, its combined gravity could draw it closer. Loose grains became part of something that could hold on to more grains. The pale bead disappeared beneath them.

A small asteroid took shape, many kilometres across. Deep inside, the bead lay pressed against dark dust and flecks of metal. Burial, impacts and warmth gradually bound the separate pieces into rock. There was enough warmth to change them, but here the whole mixture never melted. The bead kept its round edge.

Above it lay more stone than Nora's whole street could have carried.

The asteroid circled the Sun. Worlds elsewhere grew enormous. This one stayed small. Nothing rained on it; no roots prised it open. The buried rock could have remained buried through all the mornings anyone would ever count.

Then another asteroid struck.

The shock passed through the rock. Cracks found one another. A piece as big as a house broke loose, carrying the pale bead, the metal flecks and the dust that now held them together. The asteroid's gravity was too weak to pull it back.

All that gathering, and now this scattering.

But the scattered piece had a different path. Over long journeys around the Sun, the pull of planets changed it further. Other impacts chipped it down. Eventually a fragment crossed the place where Earth was travelling.

It met the air at a speed that made air a wall.

The gas ahead of it compressed and grew fiercely hot. The stone's surface melted and streamed away. A bright line opened over the sleeping town. Inside the rock, cracks widened. It broke again.

Most of the material in that bright line would never be a stone on the ground.

One small piece slowed enough for the fierce heating to stop. Its melted skin set black. It fell the rest of the way through ordinary darkness, past a cloud, past the height of the church tower, and struck the hard edge of a garden path.

A corner snapped off.

By morning it lay beside the gate, cool and silent, with a pale bead showing in the broken face.

Nora crouched.

The stone was heavier than she expected. She rubbed the grey corner with her thumb. Something inside flashed, then vanished when she turned it. She turned it back.

“Found your one?” her father called.

She looked at the hedge. The sleeping dog was barely visible. She could change her mind. She could take the striped one, which she knew, or the green one, which was probably drying out on the step.

This one was ugly on the outside. She had no name for the little round mark inside it, no idea why metal was caught there, or where its missing corner had gone.

“This one,” she said.

Her father held out his hand to help her into the van. Both her hands were full: one with the stone, the other with the empty fold of her jumper. She let the jumper fall.

At the first bend she could still see the gate. At the second she couldn't.

The stone sat in her palm. She turned it towards the window to catch the light again.
