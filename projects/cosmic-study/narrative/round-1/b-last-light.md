# Enough for Morning

The stone is small enough to close your hand around. It lies at the lip of a crater, balanced on a splinter of itself. A crack runs underneath it. Given one more hard winter, one warm afternoon, it might fall.

There have been no afternoons for a very long time.

This is one possible future. Most of the stars have finished. The great clouds that made them have thinned away, and the small stars, which outlasted almost everything, have spent their long allowance of light. Beyond this little world, the darkness contains more objects than any eye could count. It is full of things. You would need a lamp to prove it.

The stone has no lamp. It has a narrow underside that has never faced the sky, a rough upper surface, and the crack.

The world beneath it travels around two cold bodies.

Once, you could have seen those bodies glow. They formed together, gathering out of the same cloud, each drawing a portion inward. For a while their falling matter made them hot. But neither collected enough to press its heart into the lasting fire of a star. Both cooled. Their weather subsided into forms no one has seen. Their original glow dwindled beyond the reach of eyes.

People, while there were people to name such things, called them failed stars.

Imagine discovering a cupboard full of flour in a house where the oven has never been lit. Failure is an odd word for it. Almost all the hydrogen that could have fuelled their light is still there.

They circle each other in the dark.

They have done this through the deaths of suns and the long unravelling of familiar constellations. The little world circles them both, carrying its craters, its buried ice, its loose stone. Nothing here needs to know what century it is.

Yet the two bodies are coming closer.

Their movement carries energy away in tiny ripples through space. A single turn loses so little that the orbit seems permanent. Enough turns change what permanent means. The pair fall a little inward, turn a little faster, and fall again.

For ages there would be nothing worth pointing at.

Then there would be two points.

Close enough now to distort each other, the bodies raise enormous tides in material that was nearly still. Their shapes lengthen. Heat grows where the movement is resisted. They begin to show themselves, faintly, against the black.

On the crater's rim, the stone acquires a shadow.

It is a weak, confused thing. The two lights make overlapping edges. If you were there, you might put your hand down beside it, just to find out whether yours would appear too.

The lights wheel faster.

Between them, matter begins to spill. What had been held separately becomes a stream, then a widening connection. The two great bodies pull each other out of shape. There is no surface to strike like a hammer striking stone. Their outer layers meet, drag, tear free, fall back. Material flings outward. Much more stays.

For a time the sky is fierce.

Light pours into the crater. Its opposite wall appears, immensely far away; then the terraces below, then a scatter of pale stones. Heat reaches the rim. The exposed face of the little stone expands before its underside does. The old crack opens a fraction.

The splinter holds.

Above it, two bodies disappear into one swollen, churning mass.

And then the light begins to fail.

Of course it does. Falling things grow hot. Hot things cool. The new brightness has come from a collision, and even here, even now, a collision is allowed to spend itself. The crater loses its far wall. Details withdraw into the dark. The stone's shadow becomes uncertain again.

This would be a cruel place to stop the story.

It would also be too soon.

Inside the settling body, the material of both former companions presses downward together. There is enough of it left. What neither could do alone becomes possible beneath their combined weight: deep in the centre, hydrogen begins joining into helium, releasing energy.

The new body takes a long time to settle. Longer than anyone could stand beside a stone and watch. Its brightness changes as it sheds the violence of its making. But the energy leaving it now has another source, and that source has scarcely been used.

The light does not go out.

It steadies into the light of a small star.

You might have wanted something grander. Blue fire, a sky crowded with new suns, the old splendour restored. But this star is red and modest. Its light reaches a little world that remains bitterly cold. The distant dead remain dead. Nothing has turned time around.

Here, however, the far wall of a crater can be seen.

Here, warmth reaches the face of a stone.

The crack underneath has been lengthened by the first fierce heating and the cooling that followed. Now the exposed rock warms again, slowly. Different grains expand by different amounts. Along the weakened seam, a last small bridge gives way.

The stone tilts.

It falls without a sound into the crater, strikes a ledge, turns, and comes to rest against another stone. Its hidden underside faces upward. A clean patch of mineral catches the light, and for a moment the slope has a bright point of its own.

There is no one to give the star a name. No window opens. Nothing on the little world raises a face in thanks.

The light arrives all the same.

And where the stone has landed, the two stones together make a sheltering hollow, small enough for your thumb. On one side there is darkness. On the other there is light that could last longer than the universe has lived so far.

You could fit your hand around what has changed.
