# Round 4 — Current story candidates

These are alternative designs for an authored combination game, not three chapters of one story. Each keeps a different rule vocabulary. The scope is a compact story with a strong middle and ending; length should grow only if further combinations earn their place. No new artwork, simulation or full manuscript is implied by these treatments. The main recipes below are representative, not exhaustive promises of a general editor.

## A. The Harbour in the Bottle

**Player promise:** build a tiny ship, make a world around it, then let that ship carry its world.

You are making a touring exhibit in cyberspace. Visitors can enter a digital model at its own scale, as the first harbour presentation will show; being small on a workbench does not make a place unvisitable. This ordinary viewpoint change is not something the arch has to manufacture. Sera, who books the show, has commissioned two arrangements of the same work: first a harbour surrounding a bottled ship, then a touring ship displaying that whole harbour inside its bottle. Merely entering the small ship is a possible viewpoint, but does not make the second arrangement. She has a room reserved at the next stop. There is no failing server or timed rescue. The pleasure is making the exhibit, inhabiting its first arrangement, and discovering what the second can be.

### What combining means

Ordinary making creates the pieces. A transparent display bottle can hold a model at any digital scale. The fixed **Inside-out arch** in the museum’s Turning Gallery reverses the order of a complete visible enclosure chain. It never copies objects or removes their contents. The opening presents one simple before/after illustration: a box containing a cup becomes that cup containing the box. This is a taught fictional rule, not a physical claim or a new software feature.

The actual work has three unmistakable layers: **blue harbour, glass bottle, striped ship**. The harbour includes its pier and water. The ship includes its mast and light. Reversal moves each whole layer with its contents; the bottle remains the middle layer. The arch stays in the Turning Gallery, outside the assembly. It is taught in the opening but becomes an actual receiving place only after the first exhibition is complete; it is never an unusable portable tool sitting beside the unfinished ship. A finite authored recipe performs each result, with a whole composed picture; generic recursive editing is not required.

### The story, through what you make

**1. The thing that could fit in your hand.** Build a recognisable vessel from a white hull and red-striped mast. Fit a warm all-round lantern. Its bent little flag remains the visual signature all the way through. Sera calls it a very small beginning for a touring museum.

**2. A safe place for a small ship.** Enclose it in a broad clear display bottle. This is the first exhibit, not a needless rescue preparation. Its lantern shines through the glass. The new object is recognisably the ship you made, not a “saved ship” badge.

**3. The ship becomes useful to a larger place.** Make a blue harbour basin and a crescent pier with a reflective edge. Put the bottled ship into the harbour. Its lamp, shining through the clear bottle, lights the pier's approach. The work has become a place rather than a model on a shelf. The last construction shows the light reaching the familiar crescent. Visiting craft and people can appear as already commissioned exhibition activity in the result, not things the player manufactures.

**4. A place worth taking along.** Sera closes the first presentation and the complete exhibit goes to the booked Turning Gallery. The arch is an established museum facility, not a newly discovered power. The next task is to put the harbour inside its bottle aboard the ship. Visitors are outside the work during this edit; they enter the completed new presentation afterward. The three-layer arrangement is displayed plainly, so the player can reason about what becomes outside and what stays together.

**5. The harbour fits in its own former miniature.** Bring the whole harbour assembly into the fixed arch. The same striped ship becomes the outer vessel. On its open deck stands the same bottle; inside it, the blue water and crescent pier remain a complete miniature harbour. The lantern still glows beside the glass. The final scene opens aboard the made ship, ready for the booked voyage. There is no final “departure permission” combination and no new workshop silently supplied by the ending.

### Representative construction spine

| Inputs | Result | What persists |
| --- | --- | --- |
| White hull + Striped mast | Striped model ship | Bent flag, hull and mast. |
| Striped model ship + Warm lantern | Beacon ship | The same hull gains a light. |
| Beacon ship + Clear display bottle | Bottled beacon ship | Ship and lantern remain visible through glass. |
| Empty blue basin + Model seawater | Harbour basin | Blue rim and water become the outer layer. |
| Crescent pier frame + Reflective edging | Crescent pier | Its characteristic curve gains a light-catching edge. |
| Harbour basin + Crescent pier | Harbour with crescent pier | Pier belongs to the harbour layer. |
| Harbour with crescent pier + Bottled beacon ship | Lit harbour around the bottled ship | The bottled lantern now lights the pier. |
| Lit harbour around the bottled ship + Inside-out arch | Ship carrying its bottled harbour | Ship, bottle, water and pier survive once, in reversed nesting; arch remains in the gallery. |

The basin and pier can be built before the ship is bottled. The Turning Gallery is reached after the first exhibition is complete, with only the finished assembly still available. There is no advertised early two-layer operation that the game then silently refuses. This is one outward exhibition itinerary, not a free undo editor; the story ends after the chosen reversal. Other digital packings are possible in this fiction; the story does not claim this is the only way to carry data. A direct cargo packing would be another work, not a secretly forbidden obvious solution. The appeal of this particular commission is that we experienced both arrangements of the *same* work.

**Why this may carry a story.** The first creation changes from protected model, to useful beacon, to carrier of its former surroundings. Each later act changes its relationship with something already made. The ending can be seen in one cutaway. Its strongest uncertainty is whether the reversal feels delightful in play rather than like a prescribed demonstration. Eight substantial operations may already be the right size for a first prototype; do not stretch them into 200.

## B. Borrowed Moves

**Player promise:** make a way to move, then discover what else can move that way.

You are reopening a waterside route inside a neglected simulation. From the opening, the view shows a low quay, a landing directly above it, and a roof terrace across a gap at that same upper height. A warehouse blocks a low light from reaching the approach channel. Omi has already arranged a small evening gathering on the terrace; getting there and helping arrivals find it are the work, not a crowd conjured by the final recipe.

### What combining means

A **Motion comb** copies a source's displayed outward route, preserving the source. The result is a large **arrow ribbon**. Applying a ribbon to a still object copies that route into it; the ribbon remains a reusable description. Applying another ribbon to a moving object appends its outward segment and keeps earlier marked stops. At the final stop, the object waits, follows the complete route backward, and waits at its starting stop. This return behavior belongs to all motion-capable objects in the fiction; it is taught once with the initial toy lift. We are not attaching physical motors or claiming that copying a movement confers strength, light or intelligence.

Ribbons are route descriptions, not recordings of a whole repeated cycle. Thus appending Across to Up means **up, stop, across, stop; back, stop, down, stop**. It does not mean “up and down, then across and back.” A three-panel strip and three large stop marks must make this visible. Images or later animation illustrate the authored result; no player must time a crossing.

### The story, through what you make

**1. A toy's very small journey.** A discarded toy lift still goes up and comes back. Copy its Up route. Fit it to the broad platform you have made. The platform now serves the low quay and upper landing. Both ends are usable; the toy's movement has acquired a different purpose.

**2. Extend a journey instead of discarding it.** At the upper landing you can work toward the terrace that was visible all along. A toy shuttle demonstrates Across. Copy that route and append it to the lift. The same platform now stops at the upper landing, crosses to the terrace, and returns along the full route. Its original service survives as part of the longer one.

**3. Make something worth seeing.** Build a warm harbour lantern. On the low quay, the warehouse plainly hides its light from approaching craft. This was visible from the beginning; a surprise wall does not appear to force another job. The successful platform offers one working route through that space; rising alone already clears the roof.

**4. Let a creation become a source.** One attractive solution is to copy the platform's composed route into one ribbon and apply it to the lantern. The resulting travelling beacon rises above the warehouse and crosses out toward the terrace, then returns. The pattern does not supply the lantern's light; the light was made separately. The platform continues carrying visitors while its route has a second life in illumination.

**5. The same movement, differently useful.** End on the terrace with the working platform and whichever light the player made. If copied separately, they make related journeys; if attached, one creation carries both visitors and illumination; if simply raised, the light becomes visible to arrivals at each upper stop. The little toy lift still bobs beside the original quay. A way of moving that once served a toy now makes a place accessible and welcoming. The player has composed the relevant pattern rather than combined “community” with “technology.”

### Representative construction spine and alternatives

| Inputs | Result | What persists |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Deck panel + Broad safety rail | Shuttle platform | A substantial carrier; no passenger ingredient. |
| Toy lift + Motion comb | Up ribbon | Toy and comb remain; this copies a route. |
| Up ribbon + Shuttle platform | Working lift platform | Platform gains the route and its two stops. |
| Toy shuttle + Motion comb | Across ribbon | Shuttle and comb remain. |
| Across ribbon + Working lift platform | Quay-to-terrace platform | Up stop remains; Across adds a third stop. |
| Lamp + Diffusing globe | Harbour lantern | Globe spreads already existing light. |
| Quay-to-terrace platform + Motion comb | Up-then-across ribbon | Platform remains working; its route is copied. |
| Up-then-across ribbon + Harbour lantern | Travelling harbour beacon | The same route moves a different form and function. |

Accept three approaches to the light goal:

- **Raise it:** `Up ribbon + Harbour lantern → Rising harbour light`. If clearing the roof already makes arrivals visible, this completes the goal. Across is not a hidden extra requirement.
- **Let it share the longer journey:** copy the platform’s complete route and apply it, or append Across to the rising light. This makes a travelling beacon with an expressive route; it is an optional variation, not a falsely necessary engineering improvement. Applying Across first and then Up is also a valid authored alternative where the shown route clears the roof.
- **Carry it:** `Harbour lantern + Quay-to-terrace platform → Light-carrying platform`. The platform transforms into its lit state, keeping its route and visitor service; no unlit duplicate remains. The goal accepts that result. Its route can still be copied without copying its lamp.

These are different ways to reuse earlier creations: an elementary route, a composed route, or the whole carrier. The end reading and picture must match the chosen construction. The direct route must not falsely claim the player copied the platform. Goal text asks for a visible approach light, not unnecessary choreography.

The comb remains available. Applying a route preserves its reusable ribbon and copies the route into the host; it does not multiply the source toy or platform. Authoring must permit the direct and copied routes without leaving a retired duplicate platform. Only the current source, comb, route and receiver need appear together.

**Why this may carry a story.** It turns software reuse into conspicuous movement and lets a made thing become the source of another. It offers a stronger recurring construction grammar than A. Its central remaining uncertainty is presentation: the stop/append/return rule must be understood from the actual route strip. If it needs repeated technical explanation or many indistinguishable program tiles, simplify the rule or reject this direction.

## C. The Kite With Two Histories

**Player promise:** let one creation acquire different futures, then make something that remembers both.

You are making the opening work for a small festival in cyberspace. Before it opens, the work will spend time in two contributing neighbourhoods: a canal full of blue reflections and a roof garden patterned by golden shadows. Their temporary shows are booked from the beginning. The final gathering exists independently of what you make; your task is the work it will share.

### What combining means

The **Copy press** makes an independent descendant while preserving its source. Both show the same split-diamond ancestral frame. Combining two related versions makes a new descendant: their common frame appears once, while additions to different attachment areas survive together. Copying and merging preserve the earlier installed works. No people, rooms or environmental fit are copied. These are authored nonconflicting merges; this short story does not promise to solve all disagreements automatically.

The additions are things the player composes, not pre-made upgrade packs. They carry visible patterns from each visit. The final work promises a shared composition, not a magically correct navigation route or shelter for an unmeasured terrace.

### The story, through what you make

**1. One small light.** Make a luminous kite with a split-diamond frame. The unusual notch gives it a recognisable ancestry. Copy it before its first tour stop; a second version can travel to the roof garden while the first remains beside the canal.

**2. The river version.** Use a broad captured reflection pattern and a long plain tail to make a ribbon of blue waves. Fit it to the first kite. Its little diamond now pulls a long flowing shape through the canal show. The first work has changed form and acquired a specific place's mark. These are drawn patterns, not a claim that a still picture contains consciousness or live water.

**3. The roof version.** Make two broad fan panels patterned with the roof garden's angular golden shadows. Fit them to the other kite's side attachments. The common beginning now has a very different silhouette. The result is a second exhibited work, not a “public acceptance” token. Both versions remain displayed after their parts have contributed to the final work.

**4. Reunite the contributions, not the neighbourhoods.** Combine the river version with the roof version. One shared diamond remains, with both the blue tail and golden side fans. The visible operation deduplicates ancestry; it does not paste two complete kites side by side or consume the earlier shows.

**5. A beginning that can have descendants.** The festival opens with the combined work and the two earlier shows still running elsewhere. The last picture lets the player trace the notch, blue wave and gold shadow back to what they made. No final installation recipe claims to manufacture an audience. At the workbench the player edits digital works that remain displayed in their respective places; copying or merging their construction does not require hauling away those local shows. The ending is that the initial work can be changed without erasing it or choosing one descendant as the only real version.

### Representative construction spine

| Inputs | Result |
| --- | --- |
| Split-diamond frame + Luminous sail | Light kite. |
| Light kite + Copy press | Independent kite copy; source and press remain. |
| Captured blue reflection pattern + Plain tail | Blue wave-tail. |
| Blue wave-tail + First light kite | River version. |
| Captured angular shadow pattern + Plain fan panels | Golden shadow fans. |
| Golden shadow fans + Independent kite copy | Roof version. |
| River version + Roof version | Festival descendant; both earlier versions remain displayed. |

The merge output is a new design, not a copy of the physical neighbourhood. The final sculpture can be constructed from it as the complete operation, since all its components are digital. Adding the two kinds of panels directly is also coherent; merging earns its place by reusing completed versions whose independent lives the player has seen, not by an invented ban on assembly.

**Why this may carry a story.** This is a gentle story of authorship and shared ancestry, with no villain or emergency. The eye should see exactly what came from each place. Its risk is decorative shallowness: the player may experience two pleasant attachments rather than lives worth reuniting. It is the strongest alternative if expression and composition prove more enjoyable than A's scale reversal or B's route reasoning.
