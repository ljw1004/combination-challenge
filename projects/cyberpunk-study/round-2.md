# Round 2 — Three impossible things you can make

Three independent concept treatments for the existing authored combination game. Large whole creations and a small visible rule vocabulary replace fine equipment states. These are fictional cyberspace rules. Each operation below would be an explicit recipe with a composed result picture, not a general simulator. People are not operands. Narrative introductions establish purposes; the combinations must enact the important changes.

## A. The Ocean in the Hold

**Hook:** make a ship in a bottle, then make that same ship carry the ocean that once surrounded it.

You build model worlds in a tiny online workshop. You are preparing to leave its retiring host, but the model harbour you have made has become somewhere you want to keep. The solution is a reversal of inside and outside, not a larger storage box.

**Rule vocabulary:** a hollow digital object can enclose another at any scale. The **Inside-out frame** reverses one explicitly visible nesting chain. Two layers `A(B)` become `B(A)`; three layers `A(B(C))` become `C(B(A))`. The same objects survive; no copies appear. Only the finite illustrated chains are playable. Colours and cutaways show every layer. A simple reversal must establish the rule before the important one; this is not a surprise permission revealed at the ending.

| Beat | Combination and resulting work |
| --- | --- |
| A place worth entering | Glass lantern shell + Tiny garden → Garden in a lantern. |
| Discover the rule | Garden in a lantern + Inside-out frame → Garden surrounding its lantern. The former tiny interior is now the outer place; the same brass lantern stands inside it. |
| Make an inhabitant of the harbour | Empty model ship + Striped beacon → Lighthouse ship. No person is constructed; this is a working model vessel. |
| Preserve the first scale | Glass bottle + Lighthouse ship → Ship in a bottle. The stripe remains visible inside. |
| Give the exhibit a world | Ocean basin + Ship in a bottle → Ocean around the bottled ship. The blue outer basin, glass middle and striped inner hull are all visible. |
| Give that world a shore | Ocean around the bottled ship + Pier model → Harbour around the bottled ship. The pier belongs to the ocean layer, not to the bottle. |
| Carry what we made | Harbour around the bottled ship + Inside-out frame → Lighthouse ship carrying a bottled harbour. The hull becomes outermost; the complete harbour and pier remain inside the bottle in its hold. |
| Leave with it | Loaded lighthouse ship + Departure channel → Sailing workshop. The ship uses the route; this is a completed journey and new working place, not a “permission” card. |

**Causal arc.** A small creation first becomes a place we can enter. The boat then acquires a bottle and a surrounding world. That world becomes worth carrying because its harbour was made through play. The final reversal gives the first ship a second, much larger job. The ending picture contains the earlier pier and sea inside the bottle, while its beacon shines outside.

**Continuity and form.** The final operation transforms the entire one-off assembly into another complete assembly; it does not expose three independently draggable copies or need generic recursive slots. The garden/lantern is an opening lesson and remains a separate finished creation; it is not silently claimed as cargo. Four or five broad shapes suffice: lantern, garden, striped hull, bottle, blue basin, frame, with only the current few on screen.

**Risk to test.** Reversal may be an arbitrary algebra lesson. The motivation for enclosing the ship before rescuing it could feel arranged solely to enable the trick. The departure channel may be a redundant victory gesture. Does the three-layer change remain understandable from an ordinary diagram and names?

## B. Borrowed Moves

**Hook:** a toy teaches a bridge to move; the bridge's movement later becomes a lighthouse.

You make useful things in a harbour inside an old simulation. Its objects can keep their shape while receiving someone else's motion. You start with discarded demonstration toys, hoping to open a walk to the water. The place you create eventually needs to be visible from beyond its roofs.

**Rule vocabulary:** the **Motion comb** copies the complete displayed path of a source onto a broad arrow ribbon. It preserves the source. Applying a ribbon to a still object makes that object follow the path. Applying a second ribbon to an already moving object appends the second movement after its existing sequence. These are fixed authored sequences, not machine learning, a free programming language or simulated mechanics. A motion transfers only position/rotation, not light, strength, intelligence or missing structure. In this digital world the movement is executable without fitting physical motors.

| Beat | Combination and resulting work |
| --- | --- |
| First borrowed move | Rising toy piston + Motion comb → Up ribbon. The large toy visibly demonstrates the upward stroke. |
| Make a crossing | Up ribbon + Bridge deck → Lift bridge. Its two-frame picture shows the same deck across the gap and raised clear of boats. The chapter supplies its two aligned quays. |
| Discover another move | Quarter-turn toy + Motion comb → Turn ribbon. |
| Reach the second landing | Turn ribbon + Lift bridge → Lifting swingbridge. The upward stroke clears the quay before the quarter-turn aligns the deck with the other landing. Its existing route remains part of the result. |
| A creation becomes a source | Lifting swingbridge + Motion comb → Up-then-turn ribbon. The composed path can now be copied as one useful thing. |
| Make a light worth lifting | Lamp + Broad reflector → Harbour lantern. The reflector directs existing light; motion supplies no illumination. |
| Reuse the bridge's work | Up-then-turn ribbon + Harbour lantern → Rising lighthouse. It clears the roofline before sweeping its beam across the water. |
| Give the new place a return path | Rising lighthouse + Harbour entrance → Marked harbour entrance. The light is installed where its inherited movement serves arrivals; the bridge remains in use inside. |

**Causal arc.** The first crossing gives access to the waterfront. Reaching another landing demands a compound movement rather than a replacement bridge. The made bridge supplies the exact motion needed by a light behind a roofline. The closing view shows the same two movements doing different work: people cross by one, and find the crossing by the other. The original toy has not become a smarter toy; a useful pattern has acquired descendants.

**Continuity and form.** Copying makes a motion ribbon, not another source object. Applied ribbons are incorporated; the moving host remains visibly itself. No two bare ribbons combine, so the append rule has an unambiguous existing host and added movement. The current source, comb, one ribbon and recipient make a small board. Still two-frame drawings with matching path arrows must carry the prototype; an animation may help later but cannot conceal an undefined rule.

**Risk to test.** This may be a programming demonstration with a harbour painted behind it. The new landing must be an actual destination made accessible, not a surprise obstruction inserted to demand a second ribbon. The return/reset part of cyclic movement is not yet specified: one upward stroke is not automatically a continuously usable lift. The final installation might repeat a payoff already earned by making the light.

## C. The Kite With Two Histories

**Hook:** copies of your first creation acquire different lives; bringing them together keeps their shared beginning once and both new contributions.

You make a small luminous sky sculpture for a gathering in cyberspace. It proves useful in two very different places. Rather than pick one place's version as the real one, you bring their changes back into a new common work.

**Rule vocabulary:** the **Copy press** makes an independent version, leaving the source unchanged. Related versions show the same distinctive split-diamond root. Combining two versions through a **Merge loom** makes a new descendant with that common root once and additions at different attachment points intact. Originals remain where they were useful: the operation copies their construction data, not their inhabitants or their whole surrounding places. Conflicting edits are outside this short treatment, not silently solved by a magic consensus.

| Beat | Combination and resulting work |
| --- | --- |
| Make the common beginning | Split-diamond frame + Luminous sail → Signal kite. |
| Let it have another life | Signal kite + Copy press → Independent kite copy. The original remains. |
| Make a path in the dark | Original signal kite + Long blue light-tail → River kite. Its tail marks the winding approach to the waterside gathering. |
| Make a different kind of shelter | Kite copy + Gold shade fans → Sun kite. Wide side fans give a bright rooftop gathering a shaded centre. |
| Prepare the shared work | River kite + Merge loom → Loom showing the river version. The river kite itself stays in use; the loom displays its copied construction. |
| Bring back the other history | Sun kite + Loaded merge loom → Festival kite. One split-diamond core carries both the long blue tail and gold fans. |
| Use what both places taught it | Festival kite + Common terrace → A shaded terrace with a lit approach. The two additions now do their earlier jobs together. |

**Causal arc.** The original light suggests a common gathering. Its copies serve two places whose needs differ. Those experiences produce complementary changes; the closing construction is a descendant of both, while the earlier local works survive. The original crooked split remains visible, so the ending recalls a specific creation rather than presenting a generic upgraded kite.

**Continuity and form.** Source, independent copy and merged descendant are different identities; preservation is explicit. Templates are not confused with actual light sources or working shade. Separate additions fit tail versus side sockets. The short treatment must show both local uses before presenting reunion, not supply completed variants offscreen.

**Risk to test.** Why copy and merge instead of fitting both supplied additions to the original? Could the story be told just as well by welding two kits? A tail that marks a route and fans that shade a place need visible deployment, not merely longer names. The public gathering cannot exist solely because the result says “festival.” This may be a clear merge illustration with little narrative need for merging.
