# What can be combined?

Research read on 25 October 2026, in response to the new philosophy notes and the rejection of the flood dashboard. **The useful expansion is from combining named substances to handling materials, processes, arrangements and propositions.** Each must earn its place through play. Historical vocabulary is a source of experiments, not a reason to add a compulsory system.

The user's interaction requirement changes the test. Selecting a finished invention from a menu can leave the combination as background bookkeeping. In the experiments below, bringing ingredients together is the action that causes the change. Any goal, budget or pressure surrounds that gesture rather than replacing it with an instruction to manufacture an output.

## What the diet article actually argues

Rachel Laudan, **“Birth of the Modern Diet,” Scientific American, August 2000, pp.76–81**, was read in the user's six-page [local PDF](</Users/ljw/Downloads/birth of the modern diet - rachel laudan.pdf>). Full text was extracted; PDF page5/printed p.80 was also rendered and visually inspected, including the culinary-cycle illustration. The bibliographic DOI is [10.1038/scientificamerican0800-76](https://doi.org/10.1038/scientificamerican0800-76); the publisher page was not accessible through the browser tool. The local copy supplied the article, not the publisher listing.

Laudan contrasts two historical explanatory systems. The earlier courtly diet linked food preparation and digestion through cooking, heat and the balancing of bodily humors. The later account, associated with chemical physicians, explained digestion and broader living processes through fermentation and related operations. This shifted the perceived value of familiar ingredients and preparations. In her account, changes in explanatory ideas helped change elite European cuisine; the story is not simply the arrival of novel ingredients.

The crucial passages are printed pp.79–80 (PDF4–5): physicians treated digestion as fermentation rather than cooking, and fermentation was associated with putrefaction, distillation and acid/salt interactions. The figure on p.80 explicitly presents what fermentation **was believed** to explain. This is a history of theories. It does not establish that fermentation is the exclusive source of transformation in modern chemistry, or that the historical medical claims are correct. Nor is it nutritional guidance.

**Design implication:** the same material can become interesting because a player changes its process, order or conditions. Heating a mash before adding a culture need not be equivalent to heating it afterward. This is a stronger experimental departure than replacing “earth” with “grain” in the same fixed pair lookup table. The article also suggests a different kind of paradigm change: players can encounter a new explanatory model for familiar things, provided the changed rules are announced and learnable.

## Four elements, five phases, four causes

### The four are not Aristotle's invention

Aristotle himself attributes the four material elements to Empedocles in **Metaphysics I.3–4**. The primary text is readable in [W.D. Ross's translation](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/metaphysics.1.i.html), particularly the discussion of Empedocles adding earth and the later explicit attribution. This is Aristotle's account of an earlier thinker, not a surviving verbatim explanation by Empedocles. The safe formulation is that Aristotle developed an influential account of four elements, rather than first supplying the four.

For the game, the number four earns no special status from antiquity. It is a small vocabulary to test, and should be replaced if another vocabulary produces clearer or more expressive handling.

### Wuxing can foreground tendencies and relations

The **Hongfan/Great Plan** chapter of the *Book of Documents* lists water, fire, wood, metal and earth and describes their characteristic activities: descending, ascending, bending/straightening, yielding/changing and agricultural production. The primary text was read in [James Legge's translation, pp.140–141](https://original.sacred-texts.com/cfu/sbe03/sbe03031.htm). The Chinese Text Project's corresponding chapter was found in search but its full page returned403, so it was not treated as a fully read source.

This supports an experiment with tendencies and transformations rather than five interchangeable colored resources. It does not justify a simple claim that Chinese thought had exactly five primitive substances. The expert [Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy discussion of wuxing](https://iep.utm.edu/wuxing/) is secondary context: it explains the usefulness of “phases” or “processes,” while cautioning that “elements,” “movements” and other translations depend on context. The tradition is not one immutable game ruleset.

### Aristotle's causes are different explanatory questions

In **Physics II.3**, Aristotle distinguishes what something comes from, its form, the source of change, and the end for which it is done. Read in [R.P. Hardie and R.K. Gaye's translation](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/physics.2.ii.html), sections2–3. For a made object, these can prompt questions about material, arrangement, making and purpose. The source does not say a finished object must be produced by selecting one item from each of four UI categories.

The analogy is particularly useful because Aristotle's examples extend beyond physical substances: he includes letters relative to syllables and premises relative to conclusions. Thus “combination” can involve organizing parts or connecting claims. Moving from matter to ideas need not preserve a material recipe graph or invent an arbitrary association such as two doctrines automatically producing a medical school.

| Explanatory question | Manipulation worth testing | Failure to avoid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Material: what is it made from? | Bring two visible substances together; the result inherits usable properties. | A hidden noun-association catalogue. |
| Formal: what arrangement makes it this thing? | Snap, align or join pieces; geometry changes behavior. | Choosing a blueprint while the game performs the construction. |
| Efficient: what brings about the change? | Pour, culture, heat or expose a mixture; conditions visibly matter. | An extra “process” menu with no felt transformation. |
| Final: what is it for? | The same construction acquires a different use in a changed situation. | Replacing combination with an output request or a designer's single intended use. |

These mappings are design proposals. They are not claims that Aristotle anticipated this interface or that his categories measure fun.

## Three distinct experiments

These are alternatives, not features to combine into one game. Begin with the cheapest interaction that could falsify each. The current tactile workbench can test the shared gesture before any of these surrounding challenges is adopted.

### 1. Materials and form: The bridge bench

**The hand action.** Pick up two chunky pieces and bring their edges together. Contact previews whether the connection will bind, bend or separate. Release to fuse them. The resulting object remains where it formed and is itself something the player can grab and join. There is no “make bridge” button.

**The challenge.** Span a visible gap using a small set of pieces before the next load crosses. Material properties and the shape of a join are visible. A long light span and a short braced span are plausible approaches with different costs.

**The skill.** Predicting the structural consequence of a local join, then revising the construction after a small sag or crack. The combination changes geometry and function, not merely a recipe node's name.

**Small test.** Two materials, two joint behaviors, one gap, three load arrangements. Compare a pair-lookup version with an arrangement-sensitive version, using the same grab/contact/release gesture. Preserve a failed structure and let a player repair its weak point through another join.

**Falsifier.** Most contact previews require explanation, or all effective constructions are equivalent repeated beams. More pieces or a medieval theme would not cure that.

**Kill criterion.** Discard the structural implementation if the strongest strategy ignores arrangement or if its only challenge becomes precise pointer placement. It would have failed the intended combination decision even if its physics worked.

### 2. Process and transformation: The living vat

**The hand action.** Pour grain and water together, drag a culture into the mixture, and bring the vessel into contact with warmth. The material thickens, bubbles or sets in the play area. The vessel and the changing mixture are the things being handled. A contact preview communicates what heat will do before commitment.

**The challenge.** Produce enough usable food before the available oven warmth fades. One plausible route develops a fermenting mixture; another heats a simpler preparation immediately. The second buys an early portion while sacrificing a later capability. A tiny deterministic model is enough; wall-clock waiting is not required for the first test.

**The skill.** Ordering transformations and recognizing a useful intermediate state. A culture can act on a suitable mixture, while excessive heat ends its activity. Those are proposed simplified game rules and must be exposed as such, not presented as a food-safety simulation.

**Small test.** Use the same initial ingredients in two contexts, one favoring immediate heating and one favoring a cultivated intermediate. Ask players to predict the changed material and the next useful operation. Compare this with a fixed-pair recipe version rather than assuming additional simulation is better.

**Falsifier.** Players merely follow an authored order, or the process produces progress bars and waiting instead of handling. Correct predictions with no reason to choose a different sequence would also count against it.

**Kill criterion.** Discard the fermentation direction if order/conditions add mandatory chores without changing a defensible plan. Laudan's historical argument is inspiration, not a reason to retain an unsuccessful mechanic.

### 3. Ideas and inference: The disputation table

**The hand action.** Handle visible proposition fragments. Bring compatible terms together to form a claim, then combine that claim with another premise. Joining “every A is B” with “this is A” produces “this is B” through a visible, consistent inference. The player constructs the statement by touching its parts; choosing “research medicine” does not perform the work on their behalf.

**The challenge.** Make a compact explanation that accounts for a small set of observations, then confront it with a new observation. An early broad claim may be cheap and useful; a narrower construction can survive a counterexample. If this works, fictional competing schools could provide a setting. A leaderboard of historical Nobel winners is premature and would import contingent history as an apparent recipe law.

**The skill.** Tracking what follows from which assumptions, spotting a counterexample, and repairing the model without adding arbitrary exceptions. This is a different game from accumulating semantic associations between named doctrines.

**Small test.** A few simple predicates, one inference rule, and two genuinely different explanations with visible predictions. Keep a rival explanation available. Use fresh observations to distinguish logical transfer from memorized word matching.

**Falsifier.** Players join matching labels without anticipating a consequence, or they spend most of the session reading and issuing abstract goals. A correct proof is not sufficient evidence that handling the ideas is enjoyable.

**Kill criterion.** Discard the intellectual-school version if it needs many historical exceptions, controversial doctrines treated as game facts, or long text to make each join legible. Keep a simpler rule-composition experiment only if its manipulation has earned that complexity.

No specific *In Our Time* episode was supplied or identified in this research. “Ideas as the things one combines” is treated here as the user's design proposal, not a quotation or verified position attributed to Melvyn Bragg.

## What stays fixed when a level changes paradigm?

A promising comparison keeps the physical vocabulary stable: pick up, bring together, inspect contact, release, handle the changed result. It changes the causal question: material identity, process history or inferential arrangement. This lets us ask whether an earlier interaction skill transfers without pretending the whole ontology is unchanged.

Show the new local rule through a visible interaction at the beginning of a chapter. If a paradigm change merely invalidates previously reasonable combinations without a cue, it recreates the arbitrary-ontology problem. If it only changes labels while preserving the same optimal chain, it does not demonstrate meaningful freshness.

The next grader should distinguish **player-made combinations** from output instructions. Useful measurements include time to the first successful contact, invalid drops despite a displayed preview, how often the result is picked up and combined again, and whether a changed context produces a sensible changed combination. Pointer travel, fusion count and animation completion are interaction diagnostics, not pleasure scores. A fresh agent should first see the same screen a person sees; a complete rules packet cannot validate that onboarding. Visceral satisfaction and enjoyment still need actual human evidence.

## Decision for the current project

Archive the flood dashboard as an inspectable failed presentation rather than a template every future game must inherit. First test the bare combining surface. Preserve the input pieces as the most prominent objects, let the possible result appear at contact, and make the merge itself the committed action. Then select one of these challenges through evidence. A small tactile toy may teach us more at this stage than a complex simulation whose combination operation remains incidental.
