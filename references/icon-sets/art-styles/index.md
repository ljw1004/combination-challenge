# Icon art style ideas

**diderot.jpeg** - Copperplate engraving / encyclopedic illustration. The world of Diderot's Encyclopédie (1751 onward). Delicate black lines, cross-hatching, precise objects, almost scientific presentation. Particularly apt for tools, machines, architecture, and the development of technology. Diderot's great project was to document human knowledge, including the previously undervalued mechanical arts and trades. That's extraordinarily close to what our game is about. I'd simplify the engravings, perhaps introduce a few muted colours, and retain their characteristic precision.

**japanese-edo.jpeg** - Japanese Edo-period woodblock prints. Look up ukiyo-e household objects or dōgu-zukushi. Collections of everyday objects drawn with confident outlines, flattened perspective, and restrained colours. I find this particularly compelling for your game: each object looks individually interesting. What particularly appeals to me here is that a household object can have its own personality. The shapes aren't reduced to sterile symbols, but neither are they extravagantly modelled. It would allow a clay pot, lantern, pickaxe, roof tile, and window to coexist beautifully.

**medieval.png** - Medieval manuscript / marginalia. Flat perspective, whimsical proportions, little washes of colour, lively pen outlines. Particularly interesting if the player's discoveries are presented as an evolving manuscript. The risk is making everything seem medieval, even later inventions.

**modernist.jpeg** - Isotype / modernist pictograms. Associated with Gerd Arntz and Otto Neurath in the 1920s–30s. Almost entirely silhouettes, with very little detail. Extremely legible at small sizes and well suited to large collections of icons. But perhaps rather austere.

**risograph.jpeg** - Limited-palette screenprint / risograph aesthetic. This illustration by Alice Pattullo shows the kind of expressive, printed-object catalogue I have in mind. A few colours, deliberate imperfections, graphic shapes, decorative details. Modern, playful, but not the usual game-asset look. This could make the game feel like a beautifully illustrated children's nonfiction book, without looking childish. You can use colour to communicate different materials while retaining a consistent illustrated character.

**woodcut.png** - Woodcut / linocut / relief printing. Bold carved shapes, irregular marks, strong contrast, visible handmade texture. More rustic and evocative than modern flat icons. A particularly good visual language for primitive materials, huts, trees, fire, stone tools, and early crafts.

There's a potential trap with the engraved style: the originals contain far more detail than a small phone icon can support. We'd need an interpretation of engraving, rather than literal miniature engravings.

## Some thoughts on size

One possibility is rigid conformity, that every icon must be the same size (say a 56px circle).

But maybe we could branch out. Say that not every icon has to fit into an identical square, have an identical perspective, or possess an identical amount of detail. The Japanese prints and old encyclopedia plates demonstrate something interesting: objects have their own natural visual forms. A pickaxe is long and diagonal. A window is rectangular and frontal. A bowl is wide and shallow. Respecting these forms can make a collection feel far more authentic than a conventional game inventory.

If we did go with varying size, we'd still probably impose consistency in linework, palette, texture, and approximate visual weight, while allowing the compositions to vary.
