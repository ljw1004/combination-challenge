# Purpose is not another ingredient

Independent research/design pass, 28 October 2026. **Two unimplemented logical concepts, not tested games.** No UI work, publication, or human-enjoyment claim.

## What needs to change

The existing bucket probe tests end-relative fitness; Quarry adds alternative fitting means; the gate adds useful reuse. Those are defensible craft examples. Adding a fourth job would not establish a new kind of explanation. The gate's one-action solution is not a defect to repair by requiring three actions.

The sharper question is: **can play make “what happened,” “what made it happen,” and “what it was done for” come apart?** Two directions below put different parts of that question under pressure.

## Primary-source footing

- *Physics* II.3 distinguishes material, defining form, source of change, and end. They can explain the same thing without competing. II.2 gives the user knowledge of the form appropriate to an artifact's use; skilled manufacture remains necessary. [Hardie/Gaye translation](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/physics.2.ii.html).
- II.6 distinguishes a useful accidental result from the end of an action: a fallen tripod may become a seat without falling for that reason. II.8 allows an intended end not to be achieved. II.9 reasons from an end to conditions required for it, rather than treating available material as a sufficient explanation of the organized result. [Same primary text, Parts 6, 8, 9](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/physics.2.ii.html).
- *Metaphysics* VII.7 separates reasoning back from an understood end/form to an available intervention from making forward through those interventions. [Ross translation, Part 7](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/metaphysics.7.vii.html).
- *Parts of Animals* I.1 places the end first in explanation and treats earlier production as directed toward the finished thing. Its application to nature is broader than conscious human planning; a workshop cannot establish Aristotle's natural teleology. [Ogle translation, Part 1](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/parts_animals.1.i.html).

The concepts below are **design inferences**, not examples Aristotle proposed.

## Concept A — The Accidental Bell

**Logical focus:** useful consequence versus adopted purpose. This is not another “make an object fit a different material-property job” test: the important contrast is the history of why the action occurred.

### Scene 1: The painter's basin

A small riverside workshop needs a basin for washing paint brushes.

- Copper blank + forming mallet → **copper basin**.
- Place the basin at the washing bench. The brushes are washed; this job is finished.

The transition reports a concrete incidental event: while the emptied basin is drying beside the gate, a gust knocks it against the gate. Its ringing brings the keeper out. Nobody made or placed it there to summon the keeper. This event is **authored narrative evidence**, not a simulated gust or player action.

### Scene 2: Visitors at the gate

The keeper works out of sight. Arrivals need a dependable way to call, rather than hope for another gust.

- Earlier basin + suspension kit → **hanging basin gong**.
- Earlier forming mallet + gong → **visitor gong with tethered striker**.
- Place it at the visitor-side mounting point. The closing text shows a visitor striking it and the keeper answering.

The same basin's ringing occurred before, but only now is summoning a visitor's intended result. The earlier tool also acquires an intentional new role; it is incorporated as the striker, not silently duplicated. Do not add a third job merely to lengthen this.

### What each explanation contributes

- **Material:** copper can ring; wood supplies the striker. Those properties help explain the sound, not why someone installs a call device here.
- **Form:** suspension and a reachable striker organize those materials into a usable gong.
- **Efficient:** the craftsperson shapes/assembles it; the gust caused the earlier collision, and the visitor causes the later strike.
- **Final:** brush washing explains the first manufacture; calling the keeper explains the later arrangement. The accidental call does **not** retroactively become the basin's original manufacturing purpose.

### Why it might fail

The combination play remains simple. The conceptual contrast may exist only in the transition prose. If players describe it as an ordinary pot-to-bell upgrade and the historical distinction appears only after coaching, this is a short story around crafting, not the desired game. Do not “fix” that with philosophy text or a purpose-token ingredient.

**Fresh-play falsifiers:** Before using causal vocabulary, ask “Why did the keeper come out each time?” and “Why was the basin first made?” Then ask whether the call installation still had that purpose if the keeper happened to be away. Conflating useful outcome with original purpose, or success with purpose, is negative evidence. A source-blind controller can test logical legibility, not human delight.

## Concept B — A Light That Nobody Can See

**Logical focus:** diagnose the failed end without assuming the artifact is physically defective. Unlike A, this is an inspection-and-repair problem, not a historical accident contrast.

### Scene 1: Finish the workshop work

The carpenter must see the bench after dusk.

- Lamp bowl + wick-and-oil kit → **oil lamp**.
- Oil lamp + prepared protective case → **lantern**.
- Place it at the bench. The carpenter finishes the dock sign.

Use the ordinary fictional crafting compression; this is not lantern-construction guidance. The lantern is already bright and protected. Both properties remain true next scene.

### Scene 2: Find the landing

Arriving boat crews need to identify the landing from the bend. The earlier lantern is available. The visible situation supplies observations, not a requested recipe:

- The inner mounting point lies behind a solid parapet when viewed from the bend.
- The exposed outer post has an unobstructed sightline but lacks its mounting bracket.
- The lantern itself still lights the carpenter's bench normally.

The goal is **“Let arrivals see where to land,”** not “Make a brighter lantern.”

- Bracket parts + earlier workshop tool → **lantern bracket**. The tool is explicitly part of the earlier workshop kit, not an earned object; do not count it as continuity.
- Earlier lantern + bracket → **mounted lantern**.
- Place it on the outer post. Arrival is reported in completion text.

Continuity resides in the actual earlier-created lantern. If an existing safe mounting point makes the bracket unnecessary, remove that recipe and allow direct relocation. More assembly is not more reasoning.

The inner position must not report the human goal as met merely because a sound lantern was installed. Its inspectable description already explains the obstruction; no hidden geometric simulation is assumed. A literal scene map or descriptions could carry this evidence in the existing UI later.

### What each explanation contributes

- **Material:** lamp, fuel, and case support the light-producing object.
- **Form:** the lantern's organization makes it a lantern; its arrangement relative to parapet, observer, and landing makes a usable marker.
- **Efficient:** lighting the wick produces light; assembling and moving the support changes where that light is exposed.
- **Final:** enabling recognition of the landing explains which physical intervention is appropriate. Producing more light is only a possible means, not the ultimate end. A working lantern can fail this job without being a badly made lantern.

### Why it might fail

The outer post may be as immediately signposted as the gate's overhead ring. If so, accept that negative finding: this adds no meaningful depth. Two invalid placement targets are not two meaningful choices. Do not add fuel quantities, darkness timers, resource costs, extra brackets, or obscure the observations to manufacture difficulty.

**Fresh-play falsifiers:** Ask the player to inspect the scene and say what they think has to change before supplying partner help. After play: “Was the lantern broken?”, “Would making the flame brighter necessarily fix the problem?”, and “If the goal were lighting the bench again, would the outer post be better?” Evidence is an uncoached diagnosis of the relation between observer, light, and end—not completion, number of moves, or selection of the highlighted location.

## Recommendation and engine boundary

**A is the sharper philosophical probe; B is the more ordinary practical-reasoning probe. Neither earns a replacement campaign yet.** A adds the useful-consequence/intended-end distinction absent from the existing probes. B is worth keeping only if reasoning from observations rather than noun matching is perceptible in a fresh logical session.

Both can be represented with existing items, transformations, placement goals, descriptions, and stage transitions. These are content-level models: neither acoustics nor visibility is simulated. A should not acquire a new random-event system; B should not acquire a new line-of-sight engine just to validate a two-scene premise.

There is an important limit: the current grammar records combinations and placements, **not a player's intentions**. It can present an end before action and show consequences. It cannot prove that this end, rather than curiosity or recipe completion, actually explained the player's choice. If the requirement is for intention itself to change what counts as the same physical action, a new intention/commitment rule would be needed and separately justified; I do **not** recommend it now. A “purpose card” that physically transforms an object would misrepresent the target.

The useful next evidence is a frozen, source-blind logical session with no Aristotle vocabulary and no author route, preserving actions, descriptions read, help use, and spontaneous explanations. Stop at the local ending or recorded blockage. If the distinction lives only in a post-play lesson, do not multiply scenes. Keep any pleasant crafting result, but report that the stronger philosophical goal remains unmet.
